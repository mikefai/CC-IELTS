Discussion Essays: Both Sides, One Position

A task-improvement lesson for "Discuss both views and give your own opinion" questions. 3 strategies only — plus a paragraph-ordering drill and a full model answer.

Today's three strategies

1
State your position in the introduction

Band 5 essays often sit on the fence. Even while discussing both views, say which side you lean toward in your thesis sentence — "While both arguments have merit, I believe…".

2
One view per paragraph, one idea per view

Body 1 = view A + one developed idea + example. Body 2 = view B + one developed idea + example. Two thin ideas weaken a paragraph; one developed idea strengthens it.

3
Recycle the question's language, then extend it

Paraphrase the topic vocabulary in your introduction ("some people argue / others contend"), then use precise topic words in the body. Repetition of question wording costs coherence marks.

Drill: assemble a body paragraph

Some people believe children should start school at an earlier age, while others believe a later start is better. Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Click the sentences in the order they should appear in Body Paragraph 1 (the early-start view). Remember: statement → explanation → example.

Model answer (full essay)

Show the full model essay
The question of when children should begin formal schooling divides both parents and educators. While some argue that an early start gives children an academic advantage, others contend that young children benefit more from play-based learning at home. Although both positions have merit, I believe a later start better supports children's overall development. Those in favour of an early start claim that young children absorb languages and number concepts faster than older learners. A child who begins reading instruction at four, they argue, will have two extra years of practice before compulsory assessments. In countries where school begins at four, such as England, early-starters often show strong basic literacy by age seven. On the other hand, supporters of a later start point out that play is the natural engine of early learning, and that formal classrooms demand concentration skills most four-year-olds have not yet developed. German kindergarten research, for example, suggests that children who began structured schooling at six showed no long-term academic disadvantage compared with earlier starters — and reported fewer anxiety-related problems. In my view, the evidence on both sides points to a compromise: a later formal start, combined with rich, structured play in the years before school. Rather than racing into literacy drills, children at four and five build the social and attentional foundations that make later instruction effective. In conclusion, while early starters may gain short-term advantages in basic skills, a later, play-rich beginning better serves long-term development and wellbeing.

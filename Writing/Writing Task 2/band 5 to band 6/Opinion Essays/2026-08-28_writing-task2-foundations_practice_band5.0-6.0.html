IELTS Academic · Writing Task 2

Task 2 — Foundations

Your first lesson: know the question, plan it, and build four paragraphs.

ExamIELTS Academic
SectionWriting, Task 2
EssayArgument / discussion
Target band5.0 – 6.0
Time40 minutes
Minimum length250 words
1 Read the question

Five question types

Every Task 2 question is one of these five. The last sentence tells you which. Answer the exact question that is asked — not the topic in general.

TypeThe instruction usually says…Example
Opinion"To what extent do you agree or disagree?"Governments should spend more money on public transport than on new roads.
Discussion"Discuss both views and give your own opinion."Some think exams are the best way to test students; others prefer continuous coursework.
Advantages / disadvantages"Do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?"More and more people are working from home instead of in an office.
Problem / solution"What problems does this cause and what solutions can you suggest?"Many young people are leaving the countryside to live in large cities.
Two-part questionTwo direct questions, e.g. "Why…? Is this positive or negative?"People today change jobs more often than in the past.
2 The shape

Four paragraphs, always

IntroductionRewrite the statement in your own words (1 sentence). Then say what your essay will do or what you think (1 sentence).
Body 1Your first main idea. One idea only, explained and supported with an example.
Body 2Your second main idea. Same structure. For a discussion essay, this is the other view.
ConclusionRepeat your position in different words. Add no new ideas.
Two strong body paragraphs beat four weak ones At band 5–6, aim for one clear idea per body paragraph, fully explained. A list of five half-explained ideas scores lower.
3 Planning

Five minutes before you write

  1. 1 min — Underline the keywords. Write the question type next to them.
  2. 1 min — Answer the question in one sentence. That sentence is your position.
  3. 2 min — Write two body ideas. Add one example for each.
  4. 1 min — Check: does each idea actually answer this question?
4 Body paragraphs

A frame for each body paragraph

  1. Point — one sentence stating the idea. (Firstly, … / One reason is that …)
  2. Explain — why or how. (This is because … / As a result, …)
  3. Example — a real or realistic case. (For example, … / In my country, …)
  4. Link — connect back to the question. (This shows that … / Therefore, …)
Worked question

See it done

Some people believe that students should be allowed to use mobile phones in school. To what extent do you agree or disagree?
Show the 5-minute plan
Question type
Opinion (agree / disagree).
My position (one sentence)
I mostly disagree: phones cause more problems than benefits during lessons, so their use should be limited.
Body 1 — main problem
Phones distract students (messages, games, social media) → less attention, lower results. Example: a student checking notifications misses the teacher's explanation.
Body 2 — the other side, then limit it
Phones can help (quick research, dictionary, emergencies), but this can be done at set times with the teacher's permission. Example: teacher allows phones for a 10-minute research task, then they go away.
Conclusion
Restate: phones should be controlled by teachers, not freely allowed.
Show a band 6 model answer

These days, almost every student owns a mobile phone, and some people think students should be free to use them in school. In my opinion, phones cause more problems than benefits during lessons, so I mainly disagree, although I think limited use can be useful.

The main problem with phones in school is that they distract students. When a phone is on the desk, it is very tempting to check messages, play games or look at social media instead of listening to the teacher. As a result, students pay less attention and may get lower marks. For example, a student who is reading notifications during an explanation will miss important information and struggle later in the test.

On the other hand, phones are not always bad. They can be used to search for information quickly, to check the meaning of a word, or to contact family in an emergency. However, this does not mean phones should be allowed all the time. A better solution is for the teacher to decide when phones can be used. For instance, the teacher might allow phones for a short research task and then ask students to put them away.

In conclusion, I do not think students should be allowed to use their phones freely in school. Phones should be controlled by teachers so that students can benefit from them without losing their concentration.

232 words — for the real test, aim for 260–280.

Now you try

Your practice question

Some people think that children should begin learning a foreign language at primary school rather than secondary school. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Fill the plan first, then write four paragraphs. Give yourself 40 minutes.

Question type: My position (one sentence): Body 1 idea + example: Body 2 idea + example: Conclusion (restate):
Show a sample plan (one possible answer)
Question type
Opinion (agree / disagree).
My position
I agree: starting a foreign language at primary school has clear advantages, though secondary school work is still important.
Body 1
Young children learn languages more easily, especially pronunciation. Example: children who start English at age six often sound more natural than those who start at twelve.
Body 2
An early start gives more total learning time and builds confidence before exams matter. Example: by secondary school these students already have a base and can focus on grammar and writing.
Conclusion
Restate: primary school is a better starting point, and secondary school can then build on it.

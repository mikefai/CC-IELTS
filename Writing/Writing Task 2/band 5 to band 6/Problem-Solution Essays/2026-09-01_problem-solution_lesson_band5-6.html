IELTS Academic · Writing Task 2 — Problem–Solution

Problem–Solution Essays

From “what’s wrong?” to “what can we do?” — one mirrored idea per paragraph, with specific, feasible solutions.

Target band5.0 – 6.0
Time40 min · ≥250 words
Paragraphs4 always
Question cueproblems / causes & solutions
For the teacher — staging (50–60 min) + anticipated problems

Aim: Students can distinguish problem vs cause vs solution and build two mirrored body paragraphs (Problem 1→Solution 1, Problem 2→Solution 2).

Stages: 5′ lead-in (categorise traffic examples) → 8′ input (3 strategies) → 12′ controlled practice (Exercise A: sort 6 statements; Exercise B: ordering) → 15′ walkthrough (traffic worked example, weak vs strong) → 15′ production (obesity prompt, 5-min plan + 10-min paragraph draft) → 5′ peer check vs checklist.

Anticipated problems: Mixing cause and problem (e.g., “car ownership increased” is a cause, not the problem “congestion”). Fix: ask “So what? What is the bad result for people?” Solutions too vague (“government should solve it”) — push for who + what + how + cost/feasibility.

Cap: 3 strategies only. If time short, skip Exercise B and go straight to walkthrough.

Notes — what the question really asks

Problem–solution questions come in two wordings. IELTS may ask problems + solutions or causes + solutions. Both need the same 4-paragraph shape.

WordingWhat it meansExample
Problems + solutionsDescribe bad results, then fix them“What problems does this cause and what solutions can you suggest?”
Causes + solutionsExplain why it happens, then fix it“What are the causes and what measures can be taken?”
Causes + effectsVariant: causes + problems (no solutions)“What are the causes and effects?” — here Body 2 is problems, not solutions

Rule: Match the wording exactly. If it says causes, don’t drift into listing problems without linking them to causes.

Common trap
Cause ≠ problem. Cause = why it happens (more cars sold). Problem = bad result for people (roads jammed, air polluted, time wasted). Examiners reward that distinction.

Instructions — the 40-minute method

1
5 min — Plan on paper
Underline keywords, write question type, pick 2 problems (or 1 problem + 1 cause) and 1 solution for each. Write a one-sentence thesis: “This essay will discuss X problems and propose Y solutions.”
2
30 min — Write 4 paragraphs
Introduction (40–50 words, paraphrase + thesis) → Body 1: Problem 1 + Solution 1 → Body 2: Problem 2 + Solution 2 → Conclusion (30–40 words, restate + no new ideas).
3
5 min — Check
≥250 words? Each body paragraph has problem + solution linked? Solutions specific (who does what, how funded)? No new problem in conclusion?
Paragraph shape (use every time)
Point → Explain → Example → Solution → Link
E.g., “One major problem is congestion (point). This happens because car ownership has doubled while road space stayed the same (explain + cause). In Bangkok, commuters now lose 2 hours a day (example). A feasible solution is staggered work hours and a reliable bus network funded by a modest congestion charge (solution — specific). This would cut peak load and give people a viable alternative (link).”

Guidance — 3 strategies (only 3)

1
Separate problem from cause

Ask “So what?” Cause: more cars → Problem: traffic jams, lost time, pollution. Write the problem in your essay, and use the cause inside the explanation.

2
Mirror: one problem, one solution per paragraph

Body 1 = Problem A + Solution A. Body 2 = Problem B + Solution B. This creates cohesion (band 6+) without repeating ideas.

3
Make solutions specific and feasible

State who acts, what they do, how it works, and a hint of cost/feasibility. “Government should improve transport” is band 5. “The city can fund 200 electric buses with a $2 weekday congestion charge” is band 6.

How to decide
Problem = bad result people feel. Cause = reason why it happens. Solution = action that fixes it. The same topic can be any of the three depending on wording.

Exercise B — Build a band-6 paragraph (ordering)

Arrange these 5 sentences into a coherent Body 1 about congestion: Point → Explain (cause) → Effect → Example → Solution + Link. Click in the order you’d write them.

Why this order?
Point states the problem. Explain gives the cause. Effect shows the bad result. Example proves it with a place/number. Solution+Link fixes it and says why it helps. This chain is cohesion at band 6.

One major cause of congestion is the mismatch between the rapid growth in car ownership and the fixed supply of road space. In many capitals, registrations have doubled in a decade while lane kilometres have barely changed, so the same roads must absorb twice the demand at rush hour. Consequently, average commutes have lengthened sharply — for example, drivers in Bangkok now lose up to 90 minutes each way — and the delay creates wider costs such as missed appointments and higher fuel use. A feasible response is to combine a modest weekday congestion charge with employer-led staggered hours. Show full model answer (278 words, Band 6)
In many large cities, traffic congestion has become a serious daily problem. This essay argues that two linked causes — car-dependent growth and weak public transport — explain the jams, and that paired solutions can reduce them without unrealistic spending. One major cause is the rapid rise in private car ownership against a fixed road network. In numerous capitals, registrations have doubled in ten years while lane kilometres have barely expanded, so identical roads must carry twice the demand at peak times. As a result, commutes have lengthened sharply — for instance, drivers in Bangkok now lose up to ninety minutes each way — and the delay brings wider costs such as missed meetings, lower productivity and increased fuel consumption. A realistic remedy is to combine a modest weekday congestion charge with staggered work hours. Similar charges in London and Stockholm cut car entries by fifteen to twenty percent, and the revenue can fund a network of frequent, low-fare buses on dedicated lanes, giving people a viable alternative and directly easing the peak. A second cause is that existing public transport is often unreliable and inconvenient, which pushes residents toward cars even for short trips. Infrequent buses, overcrowded trains and complicated payment systems discourage use, while cycling feels unsafe on car-dominated streets. The consequence is more traffic, higher emissions and greater danger for pedestrians. Addressing this requires improving the alternative itself: prioritising buses with enforced lanes, adding around two hundred electric buses on the busiest corridors, and introducing contactless payment with short headways. Seoul’s recent reform, which lifted bus use by over twenty percent, illustrates that a better offer quickly changes behaviour. In conclusion, congestion stems mainly from more cars meeting the same roads and from underfunded transit that leaves people little choice but to drive. A small, earmarked charge that pays for better buses, together with flexible hours, tackles both causes in a feasible, fundable way. 278 words · 7 paragraphs?

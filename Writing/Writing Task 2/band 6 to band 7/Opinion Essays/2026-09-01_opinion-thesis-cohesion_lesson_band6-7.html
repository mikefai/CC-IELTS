Opinion Essays: Thesis & Cohesion

From band 6 (clear position) to band 7 (concession + cohesive development) — paraphrase, thesis, and one-idea paragraphs.

Target band6.0 – 7.0
Time40 min · 260–300 words
StanceFully agree / Fully disagree / Partly
CueTo what extent do you agree / disagree?
For the teacher — why band 6 stalls (and how this lesson fixes it)

Band 6 gap: Students write “I agree because A and B” but then list 2–3 thin reasons per paragraph, with weak paraphrase and no concession — so Task Response + Cohesion stay at 6.

This lesson caps at 3 strategies: (1) Thesis types — fully vs partly, with concession language. (2) Paraphrase the question’s content words before you argue. (3) One idea per paragraph: Point → Concede (if partly) / Explain → Example → Link.

Staging (55 min): 6′ lead-in (sort 5 opinion prompts by stance needed) → 8′ input (thesis types + paraphrase demo) → 12′ controlled (Exercise A paraphrase fix + Exercise B paragraph ordering with concession) → 15′ walkthrough (university-fees model, weak vs strong) → 12′ production (remote-work prompt) + peer checklist.

Anticipated: “I partly agree” with no clear side — fix: partly must still lean (e.g., “I largely agree, although … is valid for …”).

Notes — Opinion question types and band 7 expectations

StanceWhen to useThesis language
Fully agreeYour examples for one side are stronger and you have two distinct reasons“I completely agree that … because … and …”
Fully disagreeOpposite — you can rebut the other side“I disagree entirely; rather, …”
Partly / largely agree Band 7Both sides have merit but one is heavier overall“While … is valid in some cases, I largely agree that … because …”

Band 7 needs: a clear position throughout, concession (acknowledge the other view), and cohesion (one fully developed idea per paragraph, not a list).

If you choose “partly”, still lean
“I partly agree” with 50/50 paragraphs and no final lean reads as no position (Band 5). Partly = 60/40 or 70/30. Your conclusion must state the lean.

Instructions — 40 minutes, 4 paragraphs, always

1
5 min — Analyse + thesis
Underline content words (e.g., “university education should be free for all”) → paraphrase them in your intro → choose stance (fully/ partly + lean) → write one thesis sentence that names your two body ideas.
2
30 min — 4 paragraphs (Band 7 shape)
Intro: paraphrase (1) + thesis with lean (1). Body 1: your main reason → explain → concede one limit → example. Body 2: second reason → explain → example → link. Conclusion: restate lean, no new idea.
3
5 min — Cohesion check
Does each body = one idea? Concession present if partly? Linking words varied (while / although / however / therefore / for example) not repeated? 270–290 words?
Band 6 → 7 paragraph shape
Fully agree/disagree: Point → Explain → Example → Link.
Partly (band 7): Point → Explain → Concede (“Admittedly, … for …”) → Example that still supports your lean → Link.

Guidance — 3 strategies (only 3)

1
Paraphrase before you argue

Replace 3–4 content words: “university → tertiary, free → state-funded / no tuition fees, all students → regardless of background”. Don’t change the meaning. Band 6+ rewards it in Lexical Resource.

2
One idea, fully driven

Two strong paragraphs beat three thin ones. Each body = one claim + why + proof. If you add a second claim, start a new paragraph — or cut it.

3
Concede to show control (partly)

A short concession (“While free access can strain budgets, this is manageable if …”) lifts Task Response to 7 because you handle complexity without flipping position.

Step 3 — Model Body 1 (largely agree, 152 words, with concession)▸

A central reason to support state-funded tertiary education is that it widens access for talented students regardless of financial background. When tuition fees reach £9,000–£27,000 for a degree, many capable school-leavers from low-income families self-select out, not for lack of ability but for lack of funds. Admittedly, financing free places at scale would require substantial public spending, and this concern is legitimate in countries already facing pressure on health and school budgets. However, the evidence suggests the investment pays for itself: after Germany made public universities free in 2014, enrolment from the lowest income quartile rose by about twenty-two percent within five years, while graduate tax receipts over a career far exceed the initial outlay. The access gain therefore outweighs the fiscal cost, especially if costs are shared through a modest graduate contribution rather than upfront fees.

Show full model answer (281 words, Band 7)
Whether tertiary education should be free at the point of use remains a contested question. While the fiscal implications of state funding are considerable, I largely agree that tuition-free university is justified because it improves equality of opportunity and, over time, strengthens the broader economy. A key argument for no tuition fees is that cost is the most effective barrier to talented but poorer students. Even when loans exist, the prospect of five-figure debt deters applications from households with the lowest incomes. Admittedly, offering free places to all who qualify would increase public expenditure at a time when health and school budgets are already stretched, so the concern about affordability cannot be dismissed. Nevertheless, experience suggests the outlay is recoverable: after Germany removed fees at public universities in 2014, participation from the bottom income quartile rose by roughly twenty-two percent within five years, and higher lifetime tax revenues from graduates substantially offset the subsidy. With a ring-fenced graduate contribution rather than an upfront charge, access can be widened without an open-ended burden on general taxation. A second advantage concerns the wider economy. Countries that restrict higher education to those able to pay forgo potential productivity, because they under-use talent from a large segment of the population. By contrast, a system that selects on ability rather than wealth creates a deeper pool of engineers, nurses and teachers, whose later output raises growth and funds public services. Scotland’s decision to retain no upfront fees, while England raised them, offers a natural comparison: retention and completion among low-income entrants remained higher north of the border, with no sustained fall in teaching quality. In conclusion, although funding free tertiary education requires careful design, I largely agree that the benefits for equality and long-run economic strength outweigh the financial drawbacks, provided the model uses a modest, income-linked graduate contribution. 281 words · Thesis with lean → Body 1 partly (concede) → Body 2 fully → conclusion restates lean.

Comparatives & Superlatives

A diagnostic-first grammar lab for describing data. It finds your actual error pattern, then teaches the forms you need to select and compare key features in a chart or table.

  • 1Write comparative sentences from data that always name the point of comparison and use a single correct comparative form.
  • 2Choose as much / as many as and multiplier structures correctly for both countable and uncountable data.
  • 3Produce a short “key features” comparison for an unseen table using at least two different comparison structures accurately.

Part 3 · Form, meaning, use

The rules, with contrasts

Three core structures first (comparatives, superlatives, multipliers), then two extension structures for equivalence and framing. In Task 1 these are the tools for the overview and for selecting key features — the difference between a 5.5 and a 7.0 is doing them accurately every time, not knowing more of them.

Core 1 — Comparative + a stated referent

Form
Adjective typeComparativeExample (data)
One syllableadjective + -erhigh → higher than; low → lower than; large → larger than
Two syllables ending -y-y → -ierheavy → heavier; early → earlier
Two or more syllablesmore + adjectiveexpensive → more expensive than; significant → more significant than
Irregular—good → better; bad → worse; far → further/farther; much/many → more; little → less
Adverbs of mannermore + adverb (usually)rose more sharply than; grew faster than; fell more steeply than
In 2020, solar output was more higher than wind output. Two comparative markers (more + -er). Use one only. In 2020, solar output was higher than wind output.
The birth rate in Nigeria was considerably higher. Higher than what? A comparative always needs the second element. The birth rate in Nigeria was considerably higher than in Japan.
Use in Task 1

Comparatives carry the “key features” sentences: “Car ownership rose faster in China than in India, and by 2020 it was more than double the Indian figure.” Never leave a comparative floating without than.

Core 2 — Superlative + “the” + a scope

Form
Adjective typeSuperlativeExample (data)
One syllablethe + adjective + -estthe highest; the largest; the lowest
Two syllables ending -ythe + -iestthe heaviest; the earliest
Two or more syllablesthe most + adjectivethe most significant; the most rapid
Irregular—the best; the worst; the furthest; the least; the most
Scope (needed)+ of / among / in the groupthe highest of the six countries; the largest in Europe
Softenedone of the + superlative + pluralone of the largest economies
Finland had highest spending on education. Superlatives always take the. Finland had the highest spending on education of the six countries.
Norway had the most highest life expectancy. most + -est doubles the marker. Also: a superlative already means “more than all the others”, so you do not add higher than all the other countries. Norway had the highest life expectancy in the region.
Germany's figure was considerably the highest. considerably / much / far modify comparatives. With a superlative use by far the highest. Germany's figure was by far the highest.

Core 3 — Multipliers and countability

Form
The data is…StructureExample
An amount (uncountable: electricity, water, spending, rice)twice / three times / half as much asconsumed twice as much electricity as Factory B
A number (countable: visitors, cars, students, bottles)twice / three times as many asreceived twice as many visitors as Spain
Either, with a measured valuetwice / three times as high / as large as; or X times higher thanthe 2023 figure was twice as high as the 2013 figure
Naming the quantity nounthe amount of + uncountable · the number of + countablethe amount of waste; the number of households
Spending on rail was twice more than spending on roads. Not idiomatic. The fixed frame is as … as. Spending on rail was twice as much as spending on roads.
France received twice as much visitors as Spain. visitors is countable → as many. France received twice as many visitors as Spain.
The country produced twice as much grain than in 2010. The frame is as … as, never as … than. The country produced twice as much grain as in 2010.

Turkish L1 focus

E1 — the extra “more”. Turkish forms the comparative with -DAn daha + adjective (Almanya'dan daha yüksek). Learners carry daha across as a second marker: more higher. English allows one marker only.

E5 — the dropped “the”. Turkish has no definite article, so the disappears from superlatives: highest figure, largest country. English superlatives always take the.

E3 / E4 — “iki kat”. iki kat (“two-fold”) comes out as two times more than or twice more than. Fixed English frame: twice as much / as many as, or double.

For the teacher

How to run this

One 45-minute lab, or split across two shorter sessions. The diagnostic drives everything after it.

Staging

  1. Session A (~25 min): entry check (Part 1) → read the taxonomy together, marking what the learner missed → teach only the two or three highlighted core structures from Part 3 → Notice and Choose (Parts 4a–4b). Stop there. Homework: re-do the missed entry-check buckets from memory.
  2. Session B (~20 min): two-minute retrieval (learner states each core rule from memory, no notes) → Repair and Produce (Parts 4c–4d) → one Transfer table under light time pressure → read the dashboard and the next-step panel together.
  3. Do not teach all five structures in one sitting. Working memory holds about four new items; the extension pair (equivalence, framing) can wait for Session B or a later lesson.

Anticipated problems & solutions

  • Over-uses “more”. The learner adds more to everything (more higher, more bigger, more easier). Solution: a 60-second syllable sort — one-syllable and -y adjectives take -er; everything longer takes more; never both.
  • Superlative with no scope. “Germany was the highest.” Solution: insist every superlative ends with of / in / among + the group. Drill it as a sentence frame.
  • “twice more than” is fossilised. It resists correction because it maps directly onto the L1. Solution: over-practise the fixed chunk twice as much / many as in isolation before putting it in sentences.
  • amount vs number. The learner writes the amount of visitors. Solution: a quick sort of Task-1 nouns into countable (households, cars, students, tourists) and uncountable (electricity, water, spending, waste, rice) and pin the two collocations to the list.
  • Heavy use of “My answer also fixes this”. If the learner overrides the auto-check on most Repair/Produce items, mark those sentences yourself — the dashboard flags this.

Coaching script

Anchor: “Your English is not the problem here. You already understand the data. What is costing you marks is a handful of small grammar habits — and habits change in about a week.”

Main point: “Two rules cover most of it. One: a comparative always needs ‘than’ plus the other thing. Two: a superlative always needs ‘the’ plus ‘of the group’. Say those with me.”

If stuck on multipliers: “Do not translate ‘iki kat’. Learn the English chunk as one piece: ‘twice as much as’ for an amount, ‘twice as many as’ for a number.”

Next step: “This week, in every practice answer, underline every comparative and check it has ‘than’, and underline every superlative and check it has ‘the’ and a group.”

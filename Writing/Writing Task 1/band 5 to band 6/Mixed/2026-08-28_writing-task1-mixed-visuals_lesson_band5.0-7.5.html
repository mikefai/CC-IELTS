IELTS Academic Writing · Task 1 · Lesson 4 · Mixed visuals

Two Visuals, One Story

Some Task 1 questions give you two charts at once — a table and a line graph, a pie chart and a bar chart. A Band 5 script describes them as two separate things and stops. A Band 7.5 script treats them as one set of data with one story to tell, and spends its best sentence showing how the two connect. This lesson builds that skill in three stages.

Builds fromBand 5.0–5.5
TargetBand 7.5+
Task20 minutes · 150+ words
AssumesSingle-visual skeleton (Lessons 1–3)
New techniques3 + 4, staged
Dashboard

What a two-visual question is really asking

The instruction looks the same as always — “Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.” The only change is that the information now sits in two pictures instead of one. Your job is unchanged: one introduction, one overview, two body paragraphs, 150+ words, 20 minutes.

The Two-Visual Dilemma — why Band 5 scripts stall here

FAILURE 1

The data dump

Every number from both charts, in the order they appear, with no filtering. Two visuals doubles the data, so a dump that was merely weak on one chart becomes an unreadable wall on two. Task Achievement caps at Band 5 when nothing is selected.

FAILURE 2

The disjointed halves

Paragraph 1 is about Chart A. Paragraph 2 is about Chart B. They never meet. There is no sentence anywhere that uses the word while, whereas, or compared with to hold the two together. Coherence & Cohesion cannot rise above 5 without cross-visual links.

FAILURE 3

The missing overview

With two charts to process, the panicking candidate skips the overview entirely and goes straight to numbers — or writes an “overview” that only covers one of the two visuals. A single-visual overview on a two-visual task is an automatic Band 5 for Task Achievement.

FAILURE 4

The 50/50 clock split

Ten minutes on each chart, regardless of which one carries the real story. Often one visual is rich (a 20-year trend) and the other is thin (four categories at one point in time). Equal time forces padding on the thin one and rushing on the rich one.

The one idea this lesson turns on

Two visuals are not two tasks. They are one dataset that happens to be drawn twice. Every technique below exists to make your script sound like it was written by someone who saw one picture.

The blueprint

The universal four-paragraph plan, extended for two visuals

This is the same skeleton you learned for a single table or graph. Only paragraphs 1 and 2 change — and they change in a small, learnable way.

1

Introduction — paraphrase both visuals in one sentence

One complex sentence that names what each visual shows, the time frame(s), and the units. Not two sentences — one, joined with while or a relative clause. This is Technique A1.

Changed for two visuals
2

The dual overview — two sentences, one per visual, plus the link

Sentence 1: the single biggest feature of Visual 1. Sentence 2: the single biggest feature of Visual 2 and how it relates to Visual 1 (mirrors it, contrasts with it, or explains it). No numbers anywhere. This is Technique A2.

Changed for two visuals
3

Body paragraph 1 — the first visual (or the first shared theme)

Report and support the main features of one visual with specific figures. Use synthesis, not listing (Sub-lesson B). Default partition: one visual per body paragraph.

4

Body paragraph 2 — the second visual, linked back

Report the second visual, and include at least one sentence that ties a figure here to a figure in Body 1 (a pattern also visible in…, in contrast to…). This linking sentence is what separates Band 6.5 from Band 7.5.

Time budget for a two-visual task

2 min read both visuals and decide which carries the main story  ·  2 min introduction + dual overview  ·  7 min Body 1  ·  6 min Body 2 (shorter — it reuses language from Body 1)  ·  3 min check verbs, articles, and that the overview names both visuals.

Sub-lesson A · one 45-min session The shape

The dual-visual architecture

Three techniques. They cover only paragraphs 1 and 2 of your answer, because the body paragraphs use the same skills you already have — they just need the synthesis language from Sub-lesson B.

Technique budget this session: A1 unified introduction · A2 dual overview · A3 body partition. Stop there.

A1 · The unified introduction

The task gives you two sentences of instruction — one describing each visual. Band 5 rewrites them as two sentences. Band 7.5 fuses them into one, because a single sentence signals to the examiner that you see one dataset.

The task wording (typical)

The table below shows the amount of electricity generated by four countries in 2000 and 2020. The pie chart shows the sources of that electricity in the same four countries combined in 2020.

What most candidates write

“The table shows the electricity generated by four countries in 2000 and 2020. The pie chart shows the sources of the electricity in 2020.”

Problem: the two objects (table, pie chart) sit in separate sentences with an identical verb (shows…shows). It reads as a list, and it copies the task's structure almost word for word.
The upgrade

“The table compares total electricity output in four countries at two points in time, 2000 and 2020, while the accompanying pie chart breaks down the combined 2020 figure by energy source.”

Moves used: one main clause + while + a second clause; compares and breaks down instead of shows twice; accompanying to bind the second visual to the first; the combined 2020 figure refers back, proving you noticed the pie chart is a subset of the table.
The three-part frame for any unified introduction

[Visual 1: what + who/where + when]  +  while / whereas / and  +  [Visual 2: what + how it relates to Visual 1]. Fill the two brackets from the task wording, never copy the connector-free version.

Turkish L1 trap — the "which" chain

Learners often join the two visuals with a loose which: ✗ “The table shows electricity in four countries, which the pie chart shows the sources in 2020.” which cannot take a new subject (the pie chart). Use while / whereas for a contrast of two separate subjects; keep which only for adding information about the same noun.

✓ “…in four countries, whereas the pie chart shows the sources of that electricity in 2020.”

A2 · The dual overview

The overview is worth more than any other part of the answer, and on a two-visual task it is where most marks are lost. The rule: two sentences, each naming the biggest feature of one visual, with no numbers — and the second sentence also states the relationship between the two.

SENTENCE 1

The dominant feature of Visual 1

The one thing a person would notice from across the room: the overall direction, the biggest category, the widest gap. Begin with Overall,.

“Overall, total electricity output rose in every country over the period, with the two largest producers pulling further ahead.”
SENTENCE 2

The dominant feature of Visual 2 + the link

The headline of the second visual, then a clause connecting it to the first: a pattern that mirrors…, in contrast to…, which helps explain….

“The pie chart shows that this electricity came overwhelmingly from fossil fuels, although renewables made up a visible minority — a mix that varied little across the countries whose output had grown fastest.”
  • Three relationship types to look for — mirror (both visuals show the same pattern), contrast (they pull in opposite directions), explain (one visual is the cause or the composition of the other).
  • If you genuinely cannot see a relationship, the fallback link is scope: “…while the second chart narrows this picture to a single year.” Weak, but still a link.
  • Never put a number in either sentence. “Rose in every country” is an overview. “Rose from 120 to 180 TWh” is a body sentence in the wrong place.
Turkish L1 trap — the "-DIr" hedge

Direct translation of Turkish -DIr produces over-hedged overviews: ✗ “It can be said that generally the electricity may have increased in the countries.” An IELTS overview is a confident statement of fact about the data in front of you. Drop it can be said that, maybe, probably. ✓ “Overall, electricity output increased in all four countries.”

A3 · Choosing your body partition

You have two ways to divide the data between Body 1 and Body 2. Pick before you write — changing partway through is what produces the disjointed script.

PARTITION 1 — DEFAULT

One visual per body paragraph

Body 1 = Visual 1 in full. Body 2 = Visual 2 in full, with one or two sentences linking back. Works for every two-visual task. Safe, clear, and fast to plan.

Use it when the two visuals show different things (e.g. a trend over time + a one-year breakdown) or share no categories.

Choose this if you are unsure. It never goes wrong.
PARTITION 2 — ADVANCED

Group by shared theme, across both visuals

Body 1 = the high performers / the first half of the period, taken from both visuals at once. Body 2 = the rest, from both. Every paragraph already compares across the two pictures.

Use it only when the two visuals share the same categories (e.g. the same four countries, the same age groups). Then grouping by category is more elegant than grouping by chart.

Higher ceiling, higher risk. Attempt it once Partition 1 is automatic.
Deciding in ten seconds

Ask one question: do both visuals use the same set of names down the side? If yes → Partition 2 is available. If no → Partition 1 is your only sensible option. When in doubt, Partition 1.

Sub-lesson B · one 45-min session The sentences

Synthesis & comparison language

Four sentence-level techniques. They turn a list of numbers into prose that compares, and they give you the words that hold two visuals together. This is where Lexical Resource and Grammatical Range are won.

Technique budget this session: B1 synthesis clauses · B2 concessive/comparative bridges · B3 cross-visual links · B4 dual-subject framing.

B1 · Mechanical listing → synthesis

Two numbers that belong together should share one sentence, joined by a participle phrase (-ing / -ed) or a relative clause (which). This halves your sentence count and doubles your grammar range.

Band 5 — one number per sentenceBand 7.5 — synthesised
“Germany produced 350 TWh. France produced 340 TWh.” “Germany produced 350 TWh, closely followed by France at 340 TWh.”
“Coal was the biggest source. It was 45 per cent.” “Coal was the largest source, accounting for 45 per cent of the total.”
“The figure rose to 180 TWh in 2020. This was the highest figure.” “The figure rose to 180 TWh in 2020, the highest recorded across the period.”
“Nigeria was the lowest. It had 22 per cent.” “Nigeria, which had the lowest rate at 22 per cent, remained far behind the others.”
  • Five synthesis connectors to memorise: accounting for X · closely followed by Y · compared with Z · at X, the highest/lowest · which represented X.
  • A participle phrase has no new subject: “Coal led, accounting for 45%” — the -ing word still refers to coal. If the second fact has a different subject, use while instead (see B2).

B2 · Concessive & comparative bridges

These words carry the comparison the task explicitly asks for (“make comparisons where relevant”). Use at least three different ones across your answer.

CONCESSIVE — two things are different

while · whereas · by contrast · on the other hand

“Renewables supplied nearly a third of France's electricity, whereas in Germany the share was closer to a fifth.”

while and whereas go inside one sentence, joining two clauses. By contrast and on the other hand start a new sentence.

COMPARATIVE — one thing beats another

outpacing · lagging behind · exceeding · more than double

“Output in China grew fastest, outpacing every other country and more than doubling by 2020.”

Participle forms (outpacing, exceeding) attach to the sentence you are already in — no new clause needed.

Turkish L1 trap — comparative structure

✗ “Germany was more higher than France.” / “China grew fast more than India.” One comparative marker only: either -er or more, never both; more X than Y, not X more than Y. ✓ “Germany was higher than France.” / “China grew more quickly than India.”

B3 · Cross-visual linking sentences

At least one sentence in Body 2 must reach back to Body 1. This is the single most reliable Band 7 signal on a two-visual task — it proves you processed the visuals together, not separately.

FunctionSentence frame
Mirror — the second visual repeats the first's pattern“This concentration at the top echoes the table, where the same two countries dominated output.”
Contrast — the second visual reverses it“In contrast to the steady rise shown in the graph, the pie chart reveals a source mix that has barely shifted.”
Explain — one visual accounts for the other“This heavy reliance on coal helps account for the sharp output growth seen in the same countries in the graph.”
Scope — the fallback link“Whereas the table spans two decades, the pie chart captures only the final year, offering a snapshot rather than a trend.”

B4 · Dual-subject sentence framing

A compound sentence that names both visuals' data in one breath. Reserve this for the introduction and for one high-value sentence in the overview or the linking paragraph — overusing it makes the writing dense.

The pattern

“While [Visual 1 fact], [Visual 2 fact], suggesting [the relationship].”

“While total output climbed steadily across all four economies, the source breakdown stayed dominated by fossil fuels, suggesting that growth was met by burning more of the same rather than by switching to renewables.”

Do not turn every sentence into B4

One or two dual-subject sentences per script is a Band 7.5 signal. Five is a Band 6 signal — it reads as if you are avoiding the detail work. The body paragraphs are still mostly single-subject sentences carrying specific figures.

Sub-lesson C · two 45-min sessions The application

Guided case studies — how to run one

No new techniques here. Three worked examples that apply Sub-lessons A and B to real chart pairs, plus the drills. Each case study follows the same four steps.

Technique budget this session: 0 new. Consolidation only — A1–A3 and B1–B4 applied under time pressure.
1

Read both visuals — 90 seconds

Name the story of each in your head. Decide which one carries the main trend and which is the supporting picture.

2

Data selection — report / combine / discard

Mark the 4–6 features worth reporting, the 1–2 places the two visuals connect, and everything you will deliberately leave out.

3

Compare the three band levels

Read the Band 5.0 script and find the four failures from the Dashboard. Then read the 6.5 and the 7.5 and locate exactly what changed.

4

Write your own — 20 minutes, then self-assess

Use Drill 3's checklist. Compare against the 7.5 model, not for wording, but for whether you caught the same features and made the same links.

Case study 1 · Table + Line graph

Water: a global trend and a regional split

The line graph shows global water consumption by sector between 1900 and 2020. The table shows how water was divided between the same three sectors in three world regions in 2020. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Line graph — global water use, billion m³ per year
Agriculture Industry Domestic
Table — share of regional water use by sector, 2020 (%)
RegionAgri­cultureIndustryDomestic
North America404614
Europe325315
South Asia8875
Rows total 100%. Data is illustrative and broadly consistent with published water-use statistics.

Step 2 · Data selection

REPORT

Agriculture is the largest sector globally in every year. All three sectors rose. Agriculture: ~500 → ~2,900. Industry grew fastest in proportional terms (~40 → ~1,200, roughly thirtyfold). In the table: South Asia 88% agriculture; Europe 53% and North America 46% industry.

COMBINE

South Asia's farm-dominated split mirrors the global picture. North America and Europe reverse it — industry leads. One linking sentence in Body 2 must say this.

DISCARD

The 1950 and 1980 mid-points for industry and domestic use. Domestic figures for individual regions beyond noting they are small. The 7-point gap between North America (46%) and Europe (53%) — group them as “industry-led”.

Step 3 · The three band levels

Band 5.0 · what to stop doing

The line graph gives information about the water use in the world from 1900 to 2020 and the table is about the water use in three regions in 2020.

In 1900 the agriculture was 500 and industry was 40 and domestic was 20. In 1950 agriculture has increased to 1100 and industry to 200. In 1980 agriculture was 2100, industry was 700 and domestic was 300. In 2000 agriculture reached 2600. In 2020 agriculture was 2900, industry was 1200 and domestic was 500. So agriculture is always the biggest.

For the table, in North America agriculture is 40%, industry is 46% and domestic is 14%. In Europe agriculture is 32%, industry is 53% and domestic is 15%. In South Asia agriculture is very high with 88%, industry is only 7% and domestic is 5%.

In my opinion, South Asia uses too much water for farming and should reduce it.

168 words

  • Failure 3 — no overview. The answer goes straight from the introduction to raw numbers. “Agriculture is always the biggest” is tacked onto the end of a body paragraph, not stated up front, and nothing is said about the table's overall pattern.
  • Failure 1 — data dump. Every value from the graph is listed in order; nothing is selected. The 1950 and 1980 mid-points add no information.
  • Failure 2 — disjointed halves. Paragraph 2 is the graph, paragraph 3 is the table, and no sentence connects them. The words while, whereas, mirror, in contrast never appear.
  • Task Achievement also drops for the final sentence: “In my opinion… should reduce it” is an opinion, which does not belong in Task 1 at all.
  • Grammar: “agriculture has increased” — present perfect for a finished period (1900–1950); missing units throughout (“agriculture was 500” — 500 what?).
Band 6.5 · the realistic next step

The line graph shows global water consumption by three sectors between 1900 and 2020, measured in billions of cubic metres, while the table shows how water use was divided between the same sectors in three regions in 2020.

Overall, global water use increased in all three sectors over the period, and agriculture was always the largest user. The table shows that the balance is different in each region, because agriculture uses the most water in South Asia but industry uses the most in North America and Europe.

In 1900, agriculture used about 500 billion cubic metres, compared with only 40 for industry and 20 for domestic use. All three then rose, and by 2020 agriculture had reached around 2,900 billion cubic metres, while industry had grown to 1,200 and domestic use to 500.

In the table, South Asia is similar to the global picture, with 88% of its water going to agriculture. However, in Europe industry takes 53% and in North America 46%, which is the opposite pattern.

176 words

  • What earns 6.5: the four-paragraph shape is correct; the introduction is one unified sentence (A1); the overview names both visuals and states a relationship (A2); the body uses compared with and one synthesis clause (“grown to 1,200”); there is a linking idea (“similar to the global picture… the opposite pattern”).
  • What holds it below 7: the link is stated twice in plain words (“is similar to”, “is the opposite”) rather than woven in; “the balance is different… because” is loose; sentences stay mostly one-fact-per-clause; vocabulary is functional (“uses”, “takes”, “rose”) with little variation.
Band 7.5 · target

The line graph traces global water consumption across three sectors — agriculture, industry and domestic use — between 1900 and 2020, while the table breaks down the equivalent sectoral split for three world regions in 2020 alone.

Overall, worldwide demand climbed steadily in every sector over the 120-year period, yet agriculture remained the dominant user throughout. The regional figures, however, show that this global pattern is reproduced only in South Asia, whereas in the two wealthier regions the balance has tilted firmly towards industry.

In 1900, agriculture already accounted for roughly 500 billion cubic metres, dwarfing industry and domestic use at just 40 and 20 billion respectively. All three sectors then expanded, and by 2020 agricultural withdrawals had reached about 2,900 billion cubic metres; industry, though still far smaller, had grown the fastest in proportional terms, climbing roughly thirtyfold to 1,200 billion.

This agricultural dominance is mirrored in South Asia, where farming absorbs 88 per cent of all water and leaves industry a marginal 7 per cent. In sharp contrast, industry consumes 53 per cent of supply in Europe and 46 per cent in North America, leaving agriculture a secondary role in both.

185 words

  • Task Achievement: the overview covers both visuals and names the mirror/contrast relationship before any number; body figures are selected, not dumped (four graph values, three table values); no opinion.
  • Coherence: “this global pattern”, “this agricultural dominance” refer back cleanly; “is mirrored in South Asia” and “in sharp contrast” are the cross-visual bridges (B3), placed in Body 2.
  • Lexical: tilted, absorbs, dwarfing, withdrawals, relegating, accounted for — precise, varied, collocationally correct.
  • Grammar: participle phrases (“dwarfing…”, “climbing thirtyfold…”, “relegating…”), a semicolon linking related clauses, consistent past simple / past perfect for the finished period.
Case study 2 · Pie chart + Table

One spending pattern, four very different budgets

The pie chart shows the monthly expenditure of an average household by category in 2023. The table shows average monthly household income by the age of the main earner in the same year. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Pie chart — average household monthly spending, 2023 (%)
Housing & bills 32% Food & groceries 18% Transport 15% Other goods & services 13% Recreation & leisure 12% Savings 10%
Table — average monthly household income by age of main earner, 2023
Age of main earnerIncome (£/month)
Under 302,600
30–443,900
45–594,400
60 and over2,900
Figures are illustrative. The pie chart is a single national average and is not broken down by age.

Step 2 · Data selection

REPORT

Pie: housing largest at 32%; food 18%, transport 15%; the remaining three categories each 10–13%. Essentials (housing + food + transport) ≈ two-thirds. Table: income rises from £2,600 (under 30) to a £4,400 peak at 45–59, then falls to £2,900 at 60+.

COMBINE

The pie is a single average; the table shows the income it is applied to varies by about 70% across the life cycle. So a fixed 32% housing share is a much heavier real burden for the lower-earning youngest and oldest groups. That is the linking sentence.

DISCARD

What sits inside “other goods and services”. The exact ordering of recreation vs other goods (12 vs 13 — call them “similar”). Treating 30–44 and 45–59 as separate stories — group them as the earning peak.

Step 3 · The three band levels

Band 5.0 · what to stop doing

The pie chart shows the spending of an average household and the table shows the income of households by age.

The biggest category in the pie chart is housing and bills with 32%. After that food and groceries is 18% and transport is 15%. Other goods and services is 13% and recreation and leisure is 12%. The smallest one is savings with 10%.

In the table, people under 30 earn 2,600 pounds. People between 30 and 44 earn 3,900 pounds. People between 45 and 59 earn 4,400 pounds and this is the highest. People who are 60 or more earn 2,900 pounds.

I think young people don't earn enough and the government has to help them, and saving only 10% is not enough for the future.

161 words

  • Failure 3 — no overview. Straight from a one-line introduction into the pie chart's numbers. Nothing tells the reader the big picture of either visual.
  • Failure 2 — disjointed halves. The pie chart paragraph and the table paragraph never touch. There is no attempt to say what the two have to do with each other.
  • Failure 1 — partial data dump. Every pie slice is listed in size order with no grouping into essentials vs discretionary; every table row is read out.
  • Task Achievement: the last paragraph is pure opinion (“the government has to help”, “is not enough”).
  • Language: “people under 30 earn” — the table is about household income by age of the main earner, not what individuals earn; repetitive sentence frames (“People between X and Y earn Z”).
Band 6.5 · the realistic next step

The pie chart shows how an average household divides its monthly spending, while the table shows average monthly household income according to the age of the main earner.

Overall, most of a household's spending goes on essential costs such as housing, food and transport, and only a small proportion is saved. The table shows that income is not the same at every age, as it is highest in middle age and lower for the youngest and oldest groups.

According to the pie chart, housing and bills is the largest category at 32%, followed by food at 18% and transport at 15%. Recreation, other goods and services, and savings are all fairly close together, between 10% and 13%.

In the table, households with a main earner under 30 have the lowest income, at £2,600 per month. This rises to £3,900 for the 30–44 group and peaks at £4,400 for the 45–59 group, before falling to £2,900 for those aged 60 and over.

176 words

  • What earns 6.5: unified introduction; an overview that covers both visuals and groups the pie into essentials vs savings; the pie data is grouped (“between 10% and 13%”) rather than fully dumped; the table trend is described as a rise-peak-fall, not row by row; accurate reference to “the main earner”.
  • What holds it below 7: the two visuals are still described in separate boxes — there is no sentence explaining that the average pie applies to households with very different incomes; “income is not the same at every age, as it is highest…” is vague; limited range in the verbs (“shows”, “is”, “rises”, “falling”).
Band 7.5 · target

The pie chart sets out how a typical household divides its monthly spending across six categories, while the table records how average monthly income varies with the age of the main earner.

Overall, spending is dominated by essentials — housing, food and transport take roughly two-thirds of the budget — while only a tenth is saved. Income, by contrast, follows a clear arc across the life cycle, peaking in middle age, so this single ‘average’ in fact covers households of very unequal means.

Housing and bills form comfortably the largest expense at 32 per cent, followed by food at 18 and transport at 15. The three remaining items — other goods and services, recreation, and savings — are clustered closely together, each representing between 10 and 13 per cent of monthly outgoings.

The table explains why that fixed pattern matters. Earnings climb from £2,600 for the under-30s to a £4,400 peak at 45–59, then drop sharply to £2,900 for those aged 60 and over. For these lower-earning younger and older households, a 32 per cent housing share is a far heavier real cost than at the mid-life income peak.

182 words

  • Task Achievement: both overviews present; the “explain” relationship (an average pattern over unequal incomes) is stated in the overview and developed in Body 2; figures selected, not dumped.
  • Coherence: “by contrast”, “which means”, “the table explains why this fixed pattern matters” — the last is the cross-visual bridge (B3), and it opens Body 2 so the paragraph is built around the link.
  • Lexical: concentrated on essentials, set aside, an arc across the life cycle, clustered, climb, dropping sharply, outgoings, real cost.
  • Grammar: a dash-bracketed appositive, “while”-joined dual-subject opener (B4), a “before + -ing” participle, accurate conditionals-free factual past/present tense.
Case study 3 · Bar chart + Line graph

How you commute, and what fuel costs

The bar chart shows the main mode of transport used by commuters in two cities in 2023. The line graph shows the average price of petrol per litre between 2010 and 2023. Summarise the information by selecting and reporting the main features, and make comparisons where relevant.

Bar chart — commuters by main transport mode, 2023 (%)
Riverton Hillside
Line graph — average petrol price, £ per litre
Prices are illustrative and shown in nominal terms.

Step 2 · Data selection

REPORT

Bar: Riverton is car-dominated (62%), far ahead of public transport (20%); Hillside is led by public transport (41%), with the car close behind (38%) and cycling more than double Riverton's (13% vs 6%). Line: no steady trend — a low near £1.05 in 2016, a sharp peak of £1.85 in 2022, then a partial fall to £1.55.

COMBINE

The 2022 price spike matters far more to Riverton, where six in ten commuters drive, than to transit-oriented Hillside. Volatility in the graph translates into exposure that depends on the bar chart. That is the linking sentence.

DISCARD

The exact 2013 and 2019 prices (“drifted between roughly £1.05 and £1.40”). Walking's 12% vs 8% difference. Any attempt to explain why prices moved — Task 1 never asks for causes.

Step 3 · The three band levels

Band 5.0 · what to stop doing

The bar chart shows the transport of commuters in two cities and the line graph shows the petrol price from 2010 to 2023.

In Riverton, 62% of people use car, 20% use public transport, 6% cycle and 12% walk. In Hillside, 38% use car, 41% use public transport, 13% cycle and 8% walk. So Hillside people use more public transport than Riverton.

For the petrol price, in 2010 it was 1.20. In 2013 it has risen to 1.40. In 2016 it went down to 1.05. In 2019 it was 1.28. In 2022 it has increased a lot to 1.85 and this is the peak. In 2023 it decreased to 1.55.

I think people should use public transport more because petrol is expensive and it is better for the environment.

164 words

  • Failure 3 — no overview. No sentence states the big contrast between the two cities, or the fact that the petrol price fluctuated rather than trended. “So Hillside people use more public transport” is buried at the end of a body paragraph.
  • Failure 2 — disjointed halves. The bar chart and line graph are two separate lists. Nothing links a car-dependent city to a fuel-price spike.
  • Failure 1 — data dump. Every bar and every data point is reported; the 2013 and 2019 prices carry no weight but are still listed.
  • Task Achievement: the closing paragraph is an opinion and a recommendation — neither belongs in Task 1.
  • Grammar: “it has risen”, “it has increased” — present perfect for dated, finished points; “use car” (missing article); missing currency unit on every price.
Band 6.5 · the realistic next step

The bar chart compares how commuters travel to work in two cities, Riverton and Hillside, while the line graph shows the change in the average petrol price between 2010 and 2023.

Overall, commuters in Riverton mostly depend on cars, whereas in Hillside public transport is the most common choice. The petrol price did not follow a steady trend, but rose and fell several times before reaching its highest point in 2022.

In Riverton, 62% of commuters travel by car, which is far more than any other mode. Public transport is used by 20%, walking by 12% and cycling by only 6%. In Hillside the pattern is more balanced: 41% use public transport and 38% use cars, while cycling is higher at 13%.

The petrol price started at £1.20 per litre in 2010 and rose to £1.40 in 2013. It then fell to a low of £1.05 in 2016, recovered to £1.28 in 2019, and jumped sharply to £1.85 in 2022 before falling back to £1.55 in 2023.

180 words

  • What earns 6.5: unified introduction; the overview covers both visuals and uses whereas; the bar chart is described with comparison (“far more than any other mode”, “more balanced”); the line graph is summarised as a fluctuation with a named peak, and the mid-points are handled in one flowing sentence.
  • What holds it below 7: the two visuals still do not meet — there is no sentence saying the price spike matters more to a car-dependent city; “did not follow a steady trend, but rose and fell several times” is slightly wordy; the verbs are functional (“travel”, “use”, “started”, “rose”, “fell”).
Band 7.5 · target

The bar chart compares the main means of commuting in two cities, Riverton and Hillside, while the line graph tracks the average price of petrol over the same broad period, from 2010 to 2023.

Overall, the two cities have sharply contrasting travel habits: Riverton is dominated by the private car, whereas Hillside depends most on public transport. The petrol price, meanwhile, showed no consistent direction but fluctuated widely, spiking to its highest level in 2022 — volatility that leaves the more car-reliant city far more exposed.

In Riverton, cars account for 62 per cent of commuting journeys, dwarfing public transport at 20 per cent, walking at 12 and cycling at just 6. Hillside's profile is far more even: public transport leads at 41 per cent, closely followed by the car at 38, with cycling roughly twice as common as in Riverton.

The price of petrol drifted between about £1.05 and £1.40 for most of the period, before surging to £1.85 in 2022 and then easing to £1.55 the following year. For a city where six in ten commuters drive, that 2022 peak would have bitten far harder than in transit-oriented Hillside.

187 words

  • Task Achievement: overview names both visuals and the exposure relationship; the fluctuation is summarised, not tracked point by point; bar data is grouped and compared; no causes, no opinion.
  • Coherence: the dash clause in the overview and the final sentence of Body 2 are the two cross-visual bridges (B3); “meanwhile”, “the following year” keep the time reference clean.
  • Lexical: contrasting travel habits, dominated by, account for, far more even, drifted, surging, easing, felt far more keenly.
  • Grammar: “while”-joined dual-subject opener (B4), a participle chain in the price sentence, “dwarfing…” reduced clause, “roughly twice as common as” comparative.

Interactive drills

Drill 1

Fix the overview

Each item gives a two-visual task and a weak overview attempt. Rewrite it as a Band 7.5 dual overview: two sentences, one feature per visual, the relationship named, no numbers. Then reveal the model and the examiner note.

1Line graph: a city's population, 1990–2020 (rose throughout). Table: the city's age breakdown in 2020 (over-65s the largest group).

Weak attempt: “The line graph shows the population increased. The table shows there are more old people than young people.”
Model

“Overall, the city's population grew steadily across the three decades, while its 2020 age profile shows that this larger population is also an ageing one, with residents over 65 forming the single biggest group.”

Both visuals named · relationship = “this larger population is also an ageing one” (the two facts combined) · “this larger population” refers back · no figures.

2Bar chart: the leading electricity source in each of four countries. Pie chart: the global electricity mix (fossil fuels the majority).

Weak attempt: “Different countries use different energy sources. In the world, coal and gas are used the most.”
Model

“Overall, fossil fuels dominate the global electricity mix, and this reliance is echoed at national level, where either coal or gas is the leading source in most of the four countries shown.”

Relationship = mirror (“echoed at national level”) · “this reliance” refers back · “most of” not “3 of 4” keeps it number-free · one sentence, joined by and.

3Table: visitor numbers to five countries, 2010 and 2020 (most fell). Line graph: global tourism, 2000–2020 (long rise, sharp drop in the last year).

Weak attempt: “Tourist numbers went up in most countries but then fell in 2020 because of the pandemic.”
Model

“Overall, international tourism grew strongly for most of the period before falling sharply in its final year, and the table reflects the same reversal, with the majority of the five destinations receiving fewer visitors in 2020 than a decade earlier.”

“because of the pandemic” deleted — Task 1 never gives reasons · relationship = “the same reversal” · both visuals named · “a decade earlier” avoids a bare year-number list.

4Pie chart: the composition of household waste (organic material the largest share). Bar chart: recycling rates in six cities (a wide spread).

Weak attempt: “Food waste is the biggest part of household waste. Recycling rates are between 20% and 60% in the cities.”
Model

“Overall, organic material makes up the largest single share of household waste, yet the bar chart shows that cities differ widely in how much of their waste they manage to recycle, from a small minority in the weakest performers to a clear majority in the best.”

Relationship = contrast (“yet”) · the “20% to 60%” range is replaced by “a small minority… a clear majority” — still no numbers · “food waste” corrected to the chart's category, “organic material”.

Drill 2

Cross-visual sentence synthesis

Each item gives two isolated facts, one from each visual. Combine them into one complex sentence using a participle phrase, a relative clause, or while / whereas. Keep both figures.

1

A (line graph): Car use rose from 45% to 61% of journeys between 2000 and 2020.
B (bar chart): In 2020, car use was highest in suburban areas, at 74%.
Model

“Car use climbed from 45 to 61 per cent of journeys over the two decades, a rise most pronounced in suburban areas, where it reached 74 per cent by 2020.” Move: appositive noun phrase (“a rise most pronounced…”) + relative clause (“where it reached…”).

2

A (table): Renewables generated 22% of the country's electricity in 2020.
B (line graph): Renewable output had doubled since 2010.
Model

“Renewables accounted for 22 per cent of electricity generation in 2020, having doubled their output over the previous decade.” Move: perfect participle (“having doubled…”) attaches the second fact with no new clause.

3

A (bar chart): City X recycled 55% of its waste; City Y recycled 21%.
B (pie chart): Recyclable materials made up about 40% of total waste.
Model

“City X recycled 55 per cent of its waste compared with just 21 per cent in City Y, even though recyclable materials made up around 40 per cent of the total in both.” Move: compared with for the within-visual gap, even though for the cross-visual link.

4

A (line graph): National unemployment peaked at 11% in 2012.
B (line graph, second line): Youth unemployment peaked at 24% in the same year.
Model

“Unemployment peaked at 11 per cent in 2012, with the youth rate reaching 24 per cent — more than double the national figure — in the same year.” Move: with + noun + participle, plus a dash-bracketed comparison.

5

A (table): The average house price in the capital was £480,000.
B (bar chart): The average national house price was £270,000.
Model

“At £480,000, the average house price in the capital was close to double the national figure of £270,000.” Move: fronted “At £480,000,” + a single comparative clause holding both numbers.

6

A (pie chart): Manufacturing employed 28% of the workforce in 1990.
B (line graph): By 2020, that share had fallen to 11%.
Model

“Manufacturing, which had employed 28 per cent of the workforce in 1990, saw its share fall to 11 per cent by 2020.” Move: non-defining relative clause (“which had employed…”) carries the older figure; the main clause carries the newer one.

For the teacher

How to run this

“Mixed visuals” is one exam skill, but it decomposes into seven named techniques — more than a Band 5 student can absorb in a sitting. This page is built as three sub-lessons for exactly that reason. Teach them in order, one session each, with the technique budget stated at the top of each sub-lesson enforced.

SESSION 1 · SUB-LESSON A

The architecture

A1 unified introduction, A2 dual overview, A3 body partition. Do Drill 1 (“Fix the overview”) with the models open the first time, closed the second. Do not touch Sub-lesson B this session.

Homework: write only the introduction + overview for Case Study 1. Two sentences plus one. No body.
SESSION 2 · SUB-LESSON B

The sentences

Retrieval warm-up: reconstruct the A2 dual-overview rule from memory. Then B1–B4. Drill 2 (“Cross-visual synthesis”) is the core activity — one item modelled, the rest in pairs.

Homework: rewrite the Band 6.5 script of Case Study 2 or 3, upgrading five listing sentences to synthesis.
SESSIONS 3–4 · SUB-LESSON C

The application

Warm-up: the seven techniques by name. Then one full case study walked through steps 1–3 together, and one written under time (Drill 3) with the self-assessment. The reflection questions below close the sequence.

Homework: a second timed answer on the remaining case study, marked with Drill 3's rubric.
TURKISH L1 SPOTLIGHT

Where two visuals go wrong for Turkish speakers

Present perfect for a closed period (“the figure has risen in 2013”) — doubly common here because there are twice as many data sentences. Article omission in comparison phrases (“had highest rate”). “which” as an all-purpose join between the two visuals (see A1). “-DIr” hedging flooding the overview with “it can be said that” (see A2).

Drill: give a mixed-visual model with the linkers removed; students reinsert while / whereas / mirrored in / in contrast from a fixed list.

Where this sits in the Task 1 sequence

  1. Prerequisite, not optional. This lesson assumes the four-part skeleton is automatic on a single table, line graph, bar chart and pie chart (Lessons 1–3 of the Task 1 sequence). A student who still writes a single-visual answer without an overview is not ready — send them back one lesson.
  2. Realistic banding. The skeleton and single-visual competence sit around Band 6. Everything on this page is the 6 → 7.5 move: synthesis, cross-visual linking, precise lexis. The Band 5.0 scripts in the case studies are diagnostic anchors, not the teaching target.
  3. Process and map are not covered. Two-visual tasks are almost always chart pairs (table, line, bar, pie in combination). Diagram + diagram and map + map do exist but are rare; teach them separately if they come up.
  4. Pair with: Cambridge IELTS 17–20, Academic Writing Task 1 — look specifically for the questions that show two charts. Recent papers use them more often than older ones.

Anticipated problems & solutions

  1. The overview only covers one visual. The commonest single error. Fix: require the overview to have exactly two sentences and physically point at each chart while saying which sentence belongs to it.
  2. Students force a link that isn't there. Not every pair has a strong mirror/contrast/explain relationship. Teach the scope fallback (“whereas the table spans two decades, the pie captures one year”) so they always have a safe link and never invent a false one.
  3. Body paragraphs balloon because there is twice the data. Enforce the time budget: Body 2 is shorter than Body 1 because it reuses language. If both bodies are 70+ words the answer will run long and rushed.
  4. Partition drift. A student starts “one visual per paragraph” and slides into “by category” halfway. Fix: write the two body-paragraph topic sentences before writing the bodies.
  5. They spend the reveal comparing wording, not moves. Redirect every model comparison to: did you select the same features? did you link the visuals in the same place? Wording is the last thing to worry about.

Coaching script

CONFIDENCE ANCHOR — SAY THIS FIRST

“Two charts is not two essays. It is the same 150-word job you already do, with one extra sentence that connects the pictures. Your English is not the problem here — the plan is. We are going to fix the plan.”

THE MAIN EXPLANATION

“Paragraph 1: one sentence that names both charts. Paragraph 2: two sentences — the big thing in chart one, the big thing in chart two, and how they relate. No numbers yet. Then two body paragraphs, one chart each, and somewhere in the second one a sentence that says ‘this is like’ or ‘this is the opposite of’ the first chart. That connecting sentence is the difference between a 6 and a 7.”

IF THE STUDENT WRITES A ONE-VISUAL OVERVIEW

“Read me your overview. Now point at the chart it describes. Where is the sentence for the other chart? An overview that ignores half the task is a Band 5, however good the English.”

ONE NEXT STEP · QUICK CHECK

“This week: for every two-visual question you attempt, write the overview first and underline the word that links the two charts. If there's no linking word, the overview isn't finished.” — then check: “Give me the four relationship types.” (mirror, contrast, explain, scope)

Closing reflection — for the student

  1. In your timed answer, where exactly is the sentence that links the two visuals? If you cannot point to one, that is the single change that would raise your band most.
  2. Look at your overview. Cover the body paragraphs. Could a reader who had not seen the charts still understand the two main patterns and how they relate? If not, it is too vague or too detailed.
  3. Count your sentences that contain only one number. More than four or five, and you are listing, not synthesising — go back to B1.
  4. Which Turkish L1 trap showed up most in your writing — present perfect, missing articles, which-chaining, or over-hedging? Pick one to watch for next time.
  5. Did you keep to the time budget, or did Body 1 eat the clock? If Body 2 was rushed, your linking sentence probably suffered — and that is the expensive one to lose.

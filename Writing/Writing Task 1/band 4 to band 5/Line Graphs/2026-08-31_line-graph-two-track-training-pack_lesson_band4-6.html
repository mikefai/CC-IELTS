01 Target & five-minute diagnostic

By the end of this pack you should be able to do three things you can check yourself, without a teacher and without a score:

  1. Cover every line. Write a Task 1 answer in which each line on the graph is mentioned at least once, with the correct unit attached to every figure you quote.
  2. Produce an overview on demand. Write one sentence that names the biggest change in the whole graph, containing no specific figures, within 60 seconds of seeing a new line graph.
  3. Reach 150+ words in 20 minutes. Hit the minimum length inside the time limit on three consecutive attempts, with past simple used consistently throughout.
An honest limit Nothing in this pack awards a band. A short exercise cannot produce an official score — only a trained examiner working from a full test can. What the checks below can tell you is which specific behaviour is missing, which is the useful information anyway.

03 Teach — the Start–End–Shape check

One decision process, used on every line before you write anything. For each line, in this order:

  1. Start — what is the value at the left-hand edge?
  2. End — what is the value at the right-hand edge? Is it higher, lower, or about the same?
  3. Shape — how did it get there? Straight and steady, sharp then flat, up then down, or barely moving?

Three pieces of information per line. That is all a Band 5.5 body paragraph needs. The Start and End give you the direction, which becomes your overview. The Shape gives you the sentence that follows.

When this works

On any line graph where the lines have a recognisable direction — which covers the large majority of Academic Task 1 line graphs. It is especially strong when lines cross, because Start and End alone tell you that the order has changed.

When it does not work — and what to do instead

On a line that swings up and down and finishes where it began. Start–End says no change, which is true but misleading, because the line has clearly moved. When the Shape step tells you the line went up and down, abandon the direction sentence and describe the swing instead: it fluctuated between X and Y, reaching a peak of Z in [year]. Recognising this case is part of the skill, not an exception to it.

The graph we will work on

Graph A. The line graph below shows the average number of hours per week that adults in one country spent on three leisure activities between 2000 and 2020.

Worked example 1 — the internet line

Applying the check

Start: 2 hours in 2000. End: 24 hours in 2020 — much higher. Shape: up at every point, and the steps get bigger.

Written out: Adults spent only 2 hours a week online in 2000, but this figure rose at every five-year point and reached 24 hours by 2020. The rise became steeper after 2010.

Worked example 2 — the printed books line

Applying the check

Start: 5 hours. End: 3 hours — slightly lower. Shape: almost flat, with a small drop.

Written out: Time spent reading printed books changed very little. It stayed at around 5 hours a week until 2005 and then fell slowly to 3 hours in 2020, making it the least popular of the three activities throughout.

Note what the second example does not do: it does not give you all five figures. Two are enough, because the line barely moves. Selecting is already happening here.

The language that carries the check

Three patterns cover almost every Task 1 data sentence. Learn them as whole units, with their prepositions attached.

Verb and preposition patterns — the prepositions are not optional decoration.
PatternExampleUse it when
rose / fell from X to Y Internet use rose from 2 to 24 hours. You want to give both end points.
rose / fell by Z Internet use rose by 22 hours. You want the size of the change, not the end points.
remained / stood at X Book reading remained at around 5 hours. The line is flat, or you are fixing a single value.
there was a(n) [adjective] increase / fall in X There was a sharp increase in internet use. You want to vary the sentence shape and use the noun form.
The one that costs marks most often from … to … takes two numbers. by takes one — the difference. Writing rose by 2 to 24 hours when you mean rose from 2 to 24 hours changes the meaning: by 2 means the rise was two hours, not twenty-two.
That is three new things in this lesson: the Start–End–Shape check, the verb–preposition patterns, and grouping lines that behave alike. Nothing else is being added today, and that is deliberate.

Model answer · 176 words · written to demonstrate the target behaviours in Section 2, not to be memorised or reproduced.

The line graph shows how many people travelled by bus, underground and tram in one city between 2005 and 2025. All figures are given in millions of passengers.

Overall, bus use fell steadily across the whole period, while the underground grew and became the most popular form of transport. Tram passenger numbers changed very little.

In 2005, buses carried the most passengers, at 60 million, but this figure dropped at every five-year point and reached 34 million by 2025. The underground moved in the opposite direction. It started at 30 million, which was half the bus figure, and then rose to 45 million in 2015. Between 2015 and 2020 the two lines crossed, and by 2025 the underground was carrying almost twice as many passengers as the bus.

Tram use was much lower than the other two throughout. It began at 10 million and increased only slightly, ending at 15 million in 2025. Although this was a rise, the tram remained the least used of the three types of transport.

Why the model does what it does
  • Paragraph 1 rewords the rubric and states the units separately, which removes the temptation to copy the whole rubric in one lump.
  • Paragraph 2 is the overview: three clauses, one per line, no figures. It is written after the body in practice, but placed second on the page.
  • Paragraph 3 groups the two lines that changed and that interact — the crossing point is the single most reportable feature of this graph, so it is stated precisely as a range of years, not as an invented single year.
  • Paragraph 4 takes the flat line on its own. It gives two figures, not five, because the line does almost nothing.
  • No reasons are given anywhere. The graph shows what happened, not why.

07 Differentiate — rescue route and stretch route

Rescue — if the diagnostic falls below Band 4 behaviour

Symptoms: the learner cannot state the units, or produces no complete sentence in Q3.

  1. Hand over the Graph A data table instead of the graph. Reading the plot is not the skill being trained today; remove it as a variable.
  2. Restrict the task to one line only — the internet line — and set a target of six sentences, roughly 80 words.
  3. Supply all four sentence patterns from the table and require each to be used at least once, in any order.
  4. Only when six accurate sentences appear twice in a row, add the second line, then the third, then withdraw the table.

Expect this to take two or three lessons. Do not move on early; the 150-word target is meaningless while individual sentences are still collapsing.

Stretch — if the diagnostic already looks like Band 5.5

Symptoms: an overview appears unprompted, and Q3 and Q4 are accurate.

  1. Remove every frame and the pattern table from view before the transfer task.
  2. Cut the time limit from 20 minutes to 17.
  3. Require the crossing point to be described precisely, as a range of years, with a comparison attached — this is the sentence that separates 5.5 from 6.
  4. Require two comparative sentences using different structures, for example almost twice as many … as … and far lower than ….
  5. Ban the words increase and decrease after their first use each.

If the stretch work holds up across two attempts, move the learner to Track 2 rather than repeating this track.

08 Feedback — the three corrections worth making

A Band 4 transfer answer will contain fifteen to thirty errors. Marking all of them produces a page the learner cannot act on and usually will not read. Mark these three, in this order, and leave the rest unmarked this week.

  1. 1 · The missing overview — highest leverage by a distance

    If there is no overview, write one word in the margin — overview? — and nothing else on that paragraph. Then have the learner add it in the lesson, aloud first, on paper second. This is one sentence of work and it lifts Task Achievement more than any other single change available at this level.

  2. 2 · Verb–preposition pairs

    Underline every from / to / by / at and its verb, and mark only whether the pair is right. Do not rewrite the sentence. The learner corrects it against the pattern table. Pairs are learnable in a week; scattered vocabulary correction is not.

  3. 3 · Past simple, applied consistently

    Circle every present-tense verb and write nothing. Consistency here is a single decision made once at the start of the answer, not a hundred small ones, so it is unusually cheap to fix.

Explicitly not this week Article errors, spelling, word order in longer clauses, and range of vocabulary. All are real, none are the bottleneck, and adding them now costs the learner attention that the three items above need. Say this out loud to the learner — it stops the unmarked errors being read as approval.
What to say when returning the work

Name the behaviour, not the person, and give the next observable step: “Your three lines are all covered — that part is working. What's missing is the sentence that describes the whole graph. Add one sentence after your introduction, with no numbers in it. That's the only change I want to see next time.”

03 Teach — group, select, paraphrase

Three moves, made in this order, before a single body sentence is written. They are decisions about content, not phrases to memorise.

Move 1 — choose the grouping, and let it build the paragraphs

Look at all the lines and ask one question: what do these lines have in common, and what splits them? The most useful split on a line graph is usually direction — which lines rose and which did not. Alternatives worth considering: high values against low values, or steady lines against volatile ones.

Whichever you choose, the grouping is your paragraph structure. Two groups, two body paragraphs. A line belongs to exactly one.

When grouping by direction fails When every line moves the same way, direction gives you one group and an empty second paragraph. Switch the principle: split by rate — the lines that changed sharply against the lines that barely shifted — or by size. The move is not “group by direction”; it is “choose a principle that actually divides this graph, and say what it is”.

Move 2 — the three-fact rule

For each line, you may report:

  1. its start value,
  2. its end value,
  3. one distinguishing feature — a peak, a trough, a crossing point, a turning point, or the fact that it is the highest or lowest throughout.

Everything else stays out. On a four-line graph that is twelve facts, which is a comfortable 180 words. A Band 5 answer typically reports twenty-eight facts in 210 words and reads as a list.

“One distinguishing feature” means the feature that would be missing from the reader's understanding if you left it out. A mid-period value that sits exactly where the trend predicts is not a distinguishing feature.

Move 3 — paraphrase without drift

Rewording the rubric is not a synonym-substitution exercise. Run three checks on your version:

CheckQuestionTypical failure
ReferentAm I still describing the same people or things? four countries becomes four universities
Time frameIs the period identical, including both end years? 2006 to 2018 becomes over the last decade
MeasureIs the same thing being counted, in the same unit? students enrolling becomes students studying — a different quantity

Natural beats rare. The graph shows how many overseas students began courses… reads better than The diagram demonstrates the quantity of foreign scholars commencing tertiary education…, which is unnatural in three places at once.

Three moves this lesson: grouping, the three-fact rule, and the paraphrase checks. No fourth move is being added, because a lesson that introduces six things consolidates none of them.

Worked example 2 — applying Move 3 to the Graph A rubric

Testing two versions

The graph shows how many overseas students started university courses in four nations between 2006 and 2018, with figures given in thousands.
Referent intact · time frame intact · started courses preserves enrolling · unit stated.

The chart displays the amount of foreign students studying at universities in four countries over twelve years.
Three faults: amount for a countable noun; studying counts everyone enrolled, not those newly starting; over twelve years is arithmetically right but hides the actual dates, which the reader needs.

A free accuracy check you should be running

Before writing, interrogate the data once. Percentages of a single whole must total 100 in every year. Values described as a total must equal the sum of their parts. Where a graph gives you such a relationship, use it — it catches misreadings before they reach the page, and it costs ten seconds.

06 Review — key, model answer, and error patterns

  1. Acceptable: The graph shows what proportion of one country's people lived in cities, in the countryside and in suburbs over the four decades from 1980 to 2020.
  2. Acceptable: Overall, the population shifted decisively from the countryside into the cities, while the suburban share stayed marginal throughout.
  3. Start 5%, end 7%, and the distinguishing feature: it was by far the smallest category in every year, and barely moved. That is all three facts — a fourth would be over-reporting.
  4. Acceptable: Paragraph 1 = urban and rural, because they moved in opposite directions and the crossing between them is the story; paragraph 2 = suburban, because it is the exception that did nothing.
  5. They must total 100% in every year, because everyone lives in exactly one of the three. In 2000: 60 + 34 + 6 = 100. Correct.

Diagnostic data, for checking: Urban 45, 52, 60, 67, 72. Rural 50, 42, 34, 27, 21. Suburban 5, 6, 6, 6, 7. Urban passes rural between 1980 and 1990.

1 · Selection audit. Keep b, d, f, i and l. Those five give each of the four lines a start, an end and its distinguishing feature.

  • a, e, h, j — accurate but unremarkable. Each sits exactly where the trend already described would put it, so it adds nothing.
  • c — wrong for a different reason: it invents data. The graph has no 2015 reading. Country B was below C in 2014 (200 against 205) and above it in 2016 (250 against 215), so the crossing can only be reported as happening between 2014 and 2016.
  • g — wrong for a different reason: it is factually false. Country D ended at 130 thousand, exactly its 2006 figure, so all four is wrong. Sweeping summary sentences are where factual errors most often hide.
  • k — wrong for a different reason: it explains. Scholarships appear nowhere in the graph. Task 1 reports what the data shows; a cause is an unsupported claim.

2 · Grouping. Principle: direction. Paragraph 1 = A, B and C, the three that grew, ordered by how dramatically they did so. Paragraph 2 = D alone, the only line with no overall change. Splitting by size instead (A and C high, B and D low at the start) is defensible but weaker here, because B crosses into the top group and breaks the grouping halfway through.

3 · Faulty. The referent has changed: the rubric says four countries, the paraphrase says four universities. Everything else is fine, which is what makes this error so easy to miss when you are rushing.

4 · Faulty, in three places. Undergraduates narrows the referent — the rubric does not restrict the level of study. Quantity is used for uncountable nouns; students take number. Demonstrates does not collocate naturally with a graph presenting data; shows, gives information about or compares would.

5 · Model overview. Overall, three of the four countries attracted more international students at the end of the period than at the beginning, with the steepest growth coming from the country that had started with the fewest. The fourth country was the exception, finishing at the same level at which it began.

6 · Model repair. Country A began the period with 210 thousand students and climbed to a first peak of 265 thousand in 2010, after which enrolments slipped back for four years. Country B started from a much lower base of 90 thousand but grew without interruption, and by 2018 it had overtaken every other country with 310 thousand.

What changed: the four mechanical linkers are gone, because the relationships were already clear; it now has only one possible referent in each sentence; and the two countries are contrasted rather than merely listed one after the other.

7 · Model compression (51 words). Country C rose gradually and without interruption, from 180 thousand to 230 thousand. Country D was the only country with no overall growth: after slipping to a low of 120 thousand in 2010 and peaking at 140 thousand in 2014, it returned to its starting figure of 130 thousand.

8 · Model comparisons.

  1. In 2006, Country A received well over twice as many international students as Country B.
  2. Country B's increase of 220 thousand was more than four times the growth seen in Country C.
  3. Country D was the only one of the four whose figure was no higher at the end of the period than at the beginning.

9 · Model body paragraph 1 (86 words). The three countries that grew did so in very different ways. Country B expanded most dramatically, rising from the lowest figure of 90 thousand in 2006 to the highest of 310 thousand in 2018, passing Country C between 2014 and 2016 and Country A in the final two years. Country C's growth was far more modest but completely uninterrupted, adding 50 thousand across the period. Country A's path was less even: it climbed to 265 thousand by 2010, lost ground until 2014, and then recovered strongly to finish at 300 thousand.

10 · Model body paragraph 2 (55 words). Country D stands apart from this pattern. Its figure never moved far from 130 thousand, dropping to a low of 120 thousand in 2010 and reaching a high of only 140 thousand in 2014 before returning to its 2006 level. As a result, it fell from third place to last over the period.

Check that last claim yourself: in 2006 the order was A 210, C 180, D 130, B 90 — D was third. In 2018 it was B 310, A 300, C 230, D 130 — D was last. Claims like this are worth making precisely because they are the kind of comparison Band 6 rewards, but only if you verify them first.

Model answer · 197 words · demonstrates the Section 2 target behaviours. A worked illustration, not a template to reproduce.

The line graph compares how much electricity one country produced from coal, natural gas, nuclear power and renewable sources over the thirty years from 1990 to 2020. Output is measured in terawatt-hours.

Overall, the country moved away from coal and towards renewable energy: these two sources effectively exchanged places across the period. Gas and nuclear were far more stable, although gas finished well above its starting point.

Coal generated 180 TWh in 1990, more than the other three sources combined. Its output declined slowly at first, but collapsed after 2005 and finished at only 20 TWh. Renewables followed the opposite path. Growth was slight until 2000, after which it accelerated sharply, and by 2015 renewables had passed both coal and nuclear, going on to lead all four sources in 2020 at 140 TWh.

The other two sources moved much less. Gas rose steeply during the 1990s, from 20 to 80 TWh, and then levelled off, staying between 90 and 105 TWh for the remaining twenty years. Nuclear was the most stable of all, peaking at 70 TWh in 2000 before drifting down to 45 TWh.

Why the model does what it does
  • The grouping is by rate of change, not direction. Direction would put coal and nuclear together as “falling”, which buries the fact that coal collapsed while nuclear barely moved. Volatile against stable divides this graph honestly. That decision is the single biggest difference between this answer and a Band 5 one.
  • Twelve facts, not twenty-eight. Coal: 180, 20, and the collapse after 2005. Renewables: near-zero start, 140, and the overtaking. Gas: 20, 80–105, and the plateau. Nuclear: peak 70 in 2000, end 45, and “most stable”. Every mid-period figure that merely confirms the trend is absent.
  • Crossings are stated as ranges. “By 2015 renewables had passed both coal and nuclear” is exactly what the graph supports. Naming 2013 would be inventing a reading.
  • One verified arithmetic claim. “More than the other three combined” — 20 + 60 + 5 = 85, against 180. Claims like this earn credit; unverified ones lose it.
  • No causes. No policy, no prices, no climate targets. None of it is in the graph.
Eight error patterns to check your own answer against
PatternWhat it looks likeThe correction
1. Listing, not selecting Coal was 180 in 1990, 175 in 1995, 160 in 2000, 140 in 2005… Coal declined slowly at first but collapsed after 2005, falling from 180 to 20 TWh. Three facts replace seven.
2. Overview that covers one line Overall, coal decreased a lot during the period. Name the contrast across the graph: what rose, what fell, what stayed put. An overview about one line out of four is a detail sentence in the wrong place.
3. Paraphrase drift The graph shows the electricity used by four industries. …the electricity generated from four sources. Generation is not consumption, and a source is not an industry — two referent changes in one sentence.
4. Linkers on autopilot Moreover, gas rose. Furthermore, it levelled off. In addition, nuclear fell. Gas rose steeply during the 1990s and then levelled off, while nuclear drifted slowly downwards. Two linkers deleted, one clause subordinated.
5. Broken comparatives Coal was more higher than gas. / Renewables increased more than three times bigger. Coal was much higher than gas. / Renewables increased more than tenfold. One comparative marker per comparison.
6. Ambiguous reference Gas was above nuclear, and it continued to rise until 2020. Gas was above nuclear, and gas continued to rise until 2020. With four lines in play, repeat the noun rather than risk it.
7. Invented precision Renewables overtook coal in 2013, reaching 62.5 TWh. Renewables overtook coal between 2010 and 2015. The graph gives readings at five-year intervals only.
8. Paragraph with no grouping A paragraph containing coal, then renewables, then a sentence about gas. Ask whether the paragraph can carry a one-phrase heading. If it cannot, the lines in it do not belong together, and no linker will fix that.

07 Differentiate — rescue route and stretch route

Rescue — if performance falls below Band 5 behaviour

Symptoms: the diagnostic overview describes one line; the selection audit ticks nine statements; sentences, not decisions, are breaking down.

  1. Give the grouping instead of asking for it: “Paragraph 1 is coal and renewables. Paragraph 2 is gas and nuclear. Now write.” Judging a grouping is a separate skill from executing one; separate them.
  2. Replace the transfer graph with a three-line version — drop nuclear.
  3. Supply a bank of eight candidate facts and require exactly five to be used. Selection from a closed list precedes selection from an open graph.
  4. Extend the limit to 25 minutes for two attempts, then bring it back to 20.

If the sentences themselves are collapsing rather than the decisions, this learner belongs in Track 1 for a fortnight. Send them there; it is not a demotion, and Track 2 will not stick until Track 1's sentence patterns are automatic.

Stretch — if performance is already near Band 6

Symptoms: grouping and selection are sound; the answer is accurate but plain.

  1. Write the overview last, after both body paragraphs, and check it names a contrast neither paragraph states on its own.
  2. Cap the answer at 180 words. Tight limits force selection far harder than long ones.
  3. Ban increase and decrease after one use each, and ban Moreover, Furthermore and In addition outright.
  4. Require one sentence quantifying a proportion — almost double, around a third of, more than the other three combined — with the arithmetic verified before it is written.
  5. Cut the time limit to 18 minutes.

The proportion sentence is the highest-value stretch item: it is the clearest single signal of a writer comparing rather than listing.

08 Feedback — the three corrections worth making

Band 5 answers are usually too long and too flat, not too short. Resist the urge to correct the language first — many of the language errors in a listing answer disappear when the listing stops, because there are simply fewer sentences. Mark these three.

  1. 1 · The grouping — highest leverage

    Draw a bracket beside each body paragraph and write your one-phrase heading for it. If you cannot write one, put a question mark. Then hand it back with a single instruction: “Re-sort your lines into two groups and tell me the principle, before you rewrite anything.” This fixes paragraph structure, topic focus and a good deal of the referencing in one move.

  2. 2 · Over-reporting

    Strike through — do not correct — every sentence that reports a figure the reader could already predict. Then write one number in the margin: how many words the strikethroughs saved. Learners argue with the principle in the abstract and accept it instantly when they see that forty words vanished without any loss of meaning.

  3. 3 · One language item, chosen by frequency

    Count the language errors by type and correct only the most frequent one — usually comparative forms, or ambiguous it/this. One type, marked everywhere it occurs, so the learner sees the pattern rather than eight unrelated corrections.

Explicitly not this week Article errors, prepositions of time, spelling, and vocabulary range. Say so on the script: “I have deliberately not marked articles this week.” Otherwise the clean margin reads as a clean answer, and the learner draws the wrong conclusion.
What to say when returning the work

“Every figure in here is accurate, and that's not nothing. The problem is that paragraph two contains a rising line and a falling line, so I can't give it a heading. Sort the four lines into two groups, tell me the principle you used, and then rewrite. Don't change any of the language yet.”

Where to go next

Once these rules are consistently met on line graphs, the next useful move is a different question type rather than a harder line graph: bar charts and tables demand the same grouping and selection decisions under different data-reading pressures, while processes and maps demand a different organising principle altogether. Task 2 work — 250 words, roughly 40 minutes, and Task Response rather than Task Achievement — is a separate track and should not be started as a substitute for this one.

The Learned Song

Why a bird raised in silence can never sing properly — and what six decades of research has revealed about how song is passed from one generation to the next.

A

To a casual listener, the dawn chorus sounds like pure instinct — an automatic performance requiring no more thought than breathing. In reality, for a large proportion of the world's birds, song is a skill that must be acquired, rehearsed and refined. Of the roughly 10,000 known bird species, close to half belong to the group known as oscine songbirds, and it is these birds — thrushes, finches, sparrows and their relatives — that must learn what they sing. A young songbird removed from its own kind at hatching will still produce sound, but the result is thin, disordered and unrecognisable to members of its species. Something essential is missing, and for six decades researchers have been trying to establish precisely what.

B

The most influential account of how that gap is filled is the template model, developed and extended by Professor Halvor Lindqvist at Uppsala. In this view, a young bird passes through a sensitive phase early in life during which it listens to adults of its own species and stores a memorised model, or template, of the song. Only later does it begin to sing, and its first efforts bear little resemblance to the target. Lindqvist describes a progression from subsong — a quiet, rambling output comparable to human babbling — through a plastic stage of gradual approximation, to crystallised song, the fixed adult form that in many species will not change again.

C

Lindqvist's strongest evidence came from depriving birds of sound. Nestlings raised in acoustic isolation, hearing no adult of their species, developed songs that retained the rough species-typical rhythm but lacked its detailed structure. More striking still were birds that were surgically deafened before they began to sing: unable to hear even their own output, they produced sound that bore almost no resemblance to normal song. The conclusion was that hearing serves two distinct purposes at two distinct moments — first to acquire the model, and second to allow the bird to compare its own performance against that stored model and correct the difference. Learning, in other words, requires feedback as well as exposure.

D

Not everyone has been persuaded that the young bird arrives as a blank slate. Professor Niall Corrigan of Galway has argued that the template model understates how much is fixed before any learning begins. In his experiments, nestlings exposed simultaneously to recordings of their own species and of several unrelated species learned their own species' song overwhelmingly, even when the foreign songs were played far more frequently. For Corrigan this indicates an inherited perceptual bias — a rough sketch of what the species' song should sound like, present from hatching, which determines what the bird is willing to treat as a model in the first place. Learning, on this account, is guided rather than open.

E

A third line of work has questioned whether recordings are an adequate substitute for a living teacher. Dr Amara Osei, working in Cape Town, compared young zebra finches tutored by adult males with birds given identical songs through loudspeakers. Those with a live tutor learned faster and copied more accurately, and the difference did not disappear when the recordings were made louder or more frequent. Osei found that adult birds respond to a youngster's attempts — shifting position, calling back, ceasing to sing — and that these reactions appear to mark which attempts are closer to the target. Song learning, she concluded, is not merely acoustic but social, shaped by an interaction between two individuals.

F

If song is learned locally, it should vary from place to place, and it does. Professor Ren Takahashi of Kyoto has spent much of his career mapping the regional song variants known as dialects. In some sparrow populations, birds living only a few kilometres apart sing versions distinct enough that a trained listener can identify a bird's home valley from a single recording. Takahashi's long-term recordings show that these boundaries can remain stable for thirty years or more, far longer than the lifespan of any individual singer, because each generation copies the local version. Dialects, he argues, are cultural traditions in the strict sense: information transmitted socially and preserved across generations.

G

Cities have provided an unplanned experiment in how quickly such traditions can shift. Dr Beatriz Ferreira, working across São Paulo, has documented that birds in noisy districts sing at a higher pitch, in shorter phrases, and more often at dawn before traffic builds. Low-frequency engine noise masks the lower notes of many songs, and the birds that are heard are those that avoid that range. Ferreira is careful to note that some of this is immediate adjustment rather than learning — an individual bird can raise its pitch within minutes. But where young birds copy already-adapted adults, the change is inherited culturally, and Takahashi has suggested that some urban populations may now be forming dialects of their own.

H

The appeal of birdsong to researchers outside ornithology lies in its parallels with human speech. Both are learned from adults during a limited early window, both pass through a babbling stage, and both depend on hearing one's own output. A gene implicated in vocal learning in songbirds has a close counterpart associated with speech difficulties in humans. Yet Corrigan and Osei, from very different positions, agree that the comparison can be pushed too far: birdsong conveys identity, territory and fitness, but it does not combine elements to generate new meanings as sentences do. What songbirds share with us may be the machinery of vocal imitation rather than language itself.

Strategy guide

These three task types are the ones that most often stop a Band 6.5 candidate reaching 7.0, because all three punish the same habit: hunting for matching words instead of matching meaning.

Matching information to paragraphs (Q1–6)

  1. Do this task last, not first. It requires scanning the whole passage, so answering the other tasks first means you already know where things are. Candidates who work in printed order lose four to five minutes here.
  2. Underline the abstract noun in the question. Q1 says a comparison, Q5 says an admission, Q6 says a description. That word tells you what kind of sentence to look for, which is far more efficient than hunting the topic.
  3. Remember that letters can repeat and paragraphs can go unused. Here D and H are unused. Never assume a tidy one-to-one distribution — that assumption alone causes wrong answers.
  4. Do not eliminate a paragraph because you already used it unless the instructions say each letter is used once. Read the rubric every time; it changes between tests.

Matching statements to researchers (Q7–12)

  1. Mark every name in the passage before you read a single question. Thirty seconds spent circling Lindqvist, Corrigan, Osei, Takahashi and Ferreira converts the whole task into targeted checking.
  2. Watch for names appearing outside their own paragraph. Takahashi is introduced in F but is quoted again in G, and that second appearance is the answer to Q11. This is the single most common way this task type is made difficult.
  3. Attach the claim to the attribution verb. argues, concluded, indicates, has suggested, is careful to note — the name attached to that verb owns the claim, regardless of whose research the paragraph is about.
  4. Expect overlapping positions. Osei and Ferreira both point to environmental influence; Lindqvist and Corrigan both discuss what is present before learning. Overlap is deliberate, so a statement that sounds like two researchers means you have not yet found the distinguishing detail.
  5. An unused option is normal. Ferreira answers nothing here. If you have used every name once and have a question left, do not force a redistribution — check for a repeat instead.

Multiple-answer matching (Q13–15)

  1. The answers usually cluster. All three sit in one sentence of paragraph G. Once you find the first, read the rest of that sentence before searching elsewhere.
  2. Reject anything requiring an inference. Option B (louder singing) follows logically from masking noise, but the passage never states it. These tasks reward literal presence only.
  3. Watch for near-miss vocabulary. Option G swaps shorter phrases for longer gaps. Same semantic field, different claim.
  4. Three answers score three marks. There is no partial credit per question, but each correct letter earns its mark independently — so guess the third letter rather than leaving it blank.

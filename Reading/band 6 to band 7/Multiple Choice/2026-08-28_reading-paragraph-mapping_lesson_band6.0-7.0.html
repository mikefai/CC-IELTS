Paragraph Mapping

Strong readers do not re-read a passage for every question. They read it once, leaving behind a short record of what each paragraph did — and then answer from the record. This lesson gives you a seven-part template for that record and drills it on a full passage.

Part 1

What to record for every paragraph

A map is not a summary. A summary retells the paragraph; a map records what the paragraph is for and pins the facts a question is likely to ask about. Seven fields. Keep every entry under about eight words — notes, never sentences.

FIELD 1

Main idea

The one point the paragraph is making, in your own words. If you can only quote the passage, you have not understood it yet.

Ask: if I could keep one sentence, which?
FIELD 2

Function

The paragraph's job in the text: introduces, defines, gives an example, contrasts, explains a cause, evaluates, concludes. This is what List of Headings and matching questions test.

Ask: what is this paragraph doing, not saying?
FIELD 3

Key names

People, places, organisations, titles of works. Proper nouns are the fastest thing to relocate in a passage, so they are worth noting even when they seem minor.

Ask: which capital letters would I scan for?
FIELD 4

Dates

Years, centuries, periods, sequence words (then, later, by 1970). Questions love chronology, and a wrong date is the commonest trap in a wrong option.

Ask: what happened when, and in what order?
FIELD 5

Examples

The concrete cases the writer uses to support the main idea. Note the example and what it is an example of.

Ask: what is "for instance" attached to here?
FIELD 6

Contrasts

Any X-versus-Y in the paragraph: old vs new, claim vs counter-claim, expected vs actual. Signalled by but, however, whereas, unlike, rather than.

Ask: what is being set against what?
FIELD 7

Causes & effects

What led to what. Signalled by because, so, as a result, led to, meant that. Write it as an arrow: cause → effect.

Ask: what is the chain of consequence?
THE HABIT

Map as you read, once

Fill the fields the moment you finish each paragraph — not at the end, not on a second pass. A field can be blank if the paragraph genuinely has nothing for it. Blank is information too.

Not every paragraph fills all seven.

One paragraph, fully mapped

Read the paragraph below, then look at the completed map. The right-hand notes in mono explain the choice — they are not part of the map itself.

Before 1840, sending a letter in Britain was expensive, and the recipient, not the sender, usually paid. Charges depended on distance and on the number of sheets, so a single letter travelling the length of the country could cost a labourer a day's wage. In 1837 Rowland Hill, a schoolteacher and civil servant, published a pamphlet arguing that high prices actually reduced the Post Office's income, because they discouraged people from writing at all. He proposed a flat charge of one penny for any letter under half an ounce, paid in advance by means of an adhesive stamp. The reform was introduced in 1840. Letter volumes doubled within a year and had trebled by 1850, and other countries copied the system within a decade.

Part 3 · Reading passage

The Unglamorous Revolution

Read the passage once. Paragraphs are labelled A–H. Do not answer any questions yet — you will map it first.

The Unglamorous Revolution
A

For most of maritime history, loading a ship was slow, expensive and dangerous. Cargo arrived at the docks as a jumble of barrels, sacks, crates and loose machinery, and gangs of dockworkers stowed each item by hand, fitting it into the hold like the pieces of a puzzle. A medium-sized freighter might spend a week or more in port for every few days at sea. Labour accounted for much of the total cost of shipping, pilferage from opened crates was routine, and a manufacturer in the American Midwest could rarely calculate in advance what it would cost to send goods to Europe. Ocean transport was a bottleneck that shaped what was worth trading at all.

B

The person usually credited with changing this was Malcom McLean, the owner of a large American trucking company. McLean was not an engineer and had no background in shipping. His interest was practical: his trucks wasted hours waiting on congested piers while their loads were transferred by hand. In 1955 he bought a small shipping line, and in April 1956 a converted tanker named the Ideal-X sailed from Newark, New Jersey, to Houston, Texas, carrying fifty-eight sealed metal boxes on a reinforced deck. The boxes were lifted straight onto waiting truck chassis. The cost of loading loose cargo, McLean later calculated, fell from several dollars a ton to a few cents.

C

The metal box itself was not the invention. Railway companies in Britain and the United States had moved goods in wooden containers since the nineteenth century, and the American army used standard boxes during the Second World War. What McLean grasped was that the container was useless on its own. Savings came only when the box, the ship, the crane, the truck trailer and the loading dock were all redesigned around one another as a single system. A box that did not fit the cranes, or that road-transport rules would not allow onto a highway, saved nobody anything. This systemic view, rather than the container, was the real break with the past.

D

For the system to work beyond a single company, the boxes had to be identical everywhere. Through the 1960s, shipping lines, railways and governments argued over dimensions, corner fittings and locking mechanisms, each favouring the standard that matched its own existing equipment. Agreement came slowly, through the International Organization for Standardization, which by 1970 had settled on the twenty- and forty-foot lengths still in use today. Only then could a container from any manufacturer be stacked on any ship and lifted by any crane. The economic value of a standard, in other words, lay less in its technical merits than in the fact that everyone accepted the same one.

E

Containers also broke the world of dock labour. A traditional pier employed hundreds of men to carry cargo; a container terminal needed a handful of crane operators and clerks. Dockworkers' unions in New York, London and elsewhere fought the change through strikes and negotiation. In the United States, a 1960 agreement traded acceptance of mechanisation for guaranteed wages and a fund financed by a fee on every container. Employment on the New York waterfront nonetheless fell by more than two-thirds within fifteen years. The old riverside districts that had lived off the docks declined sharply as the work moved elsewhere.

F

An unexpected boost came from war. In 1967 the United States military, struggling to supply its forces in Vietnam through a single overwhelmed port, awarded McLean's company a contract to run a container service to Cam Ranh Bay. The operation worked so well that the ships would otherwise have crossed the Pacific empty on the return leg. Instead, McLean's vessels called at Japan on the way home and picked up cargo from its rapidly growing electronics industry. A trade route that had been an afterthought became one of the busiest in the world, and containerised goods from Asia began arriving in American stores in quantity.

G

The container also remade the geography of ports. The cramped finger-piers of Manhattan and the enclosed docks of inner London had no room for the acres of paved yard that stacking containers required, nor the deep water that ever-larger ships demanded. Traffic shifted to sites with space and depth: Newark rather than New York, the small Suffolk harbour of Felixstowe rather than London, Rotterdam rather than the older Dutch ports. Cities that had been shipping centres for centuries lost their working waterfronts within a generation, while places few people had heard of became gateways for entire national economies.

H

The wider consequences are still debated. Sea freight became so cheap that its cost almost dropped out of companies' decisions about where to make things, and manufacturing spread to wherever labour was cheapest, linked back to consumers by long supply chains. One study, by the economists Daniel Bernhofen, Zouheir El-Beze and David Kneller, estimated that containerisation did more to increase trade among industrialised countries between 1962 and 1990 than all the trade agreements signed in the same period. The box is now so ordinary that it has become invisible, which is perhaps the clearest sign of how completely it succeeded.

~840 words · The people, dates and the study described are historically accurate.

12:00 Not started.
Why ninety seconds

That is training pace. In the real test you get roughly twenty minutes for a whole passage and its questions, so nobody writes a seven-field grid under exam conditions. The grid trains your eye now; on test day it shrinks to a three-to-five word note in the margin. Part 5 is where you find out which fields you can fill fast.

Marking note

Twelve questions is a technique check, not a band score — a full Academic Reading paper is three passages and forty questions in sixty minutes. Read the misses diagnostically: headings wrong → work the Function field; matching-information wrong → work Key names, Dates, Examples; the multiple-choice wrong → work Contrasts and Causes & effects.

Reflection

Six questions about your own reading

Answer these before you move on. They matter more than the score.

  1. Which of the seven fields did you leave blank most often? What does that suggest about what you notice and what you skip while reading?
  2. Look at your Main idea notes. Are they in your own words, or phrases lifted straight from the passage? Lifted phrases usually mean you were copying, not understanding.
  3. For which paragraphs was Function hard to name? Those are exactly the paragraphs a List of Headings question would catch you on.
  4. Did filling Contrasts and Causes & effects change how fast you found the answers to Questions 11 and 12? Compare with how you normally do multiple choice.
  5. Could you map one paragraph in ninety seconds? If not, which fields slowed you down — and which two or three would you keep if you had only forty seconds?
  6. Where did your map disagree with the model map? For each disagreement, decide: did you miss it, or did you record something the passage does not actually say?
For the teacher

How to run this

Paragraph mapping is one technique, but its template has seven fields — more than working memory can deploy at once. Do not teach all seven in a single lesson: students will produce seven shallow columns and consolidate none of them. Stage it across three sessions, adding fields in the order they pay off.

SESSION 1

Main idea + Function

These two drive List of Headings and matching-information — the highest-value question types. Map all eight paragraphs with just these two fields, then do Questions 1–5 with the key open.

Homework: write a one-line function label for every paragraph of any Cambridge passage.
SESSION 2

Add Key names, Dates, Examples

Retrieval warm-up: name the function of each paragraph from memory. Then re-map adding the three "locate fast" fields, and do Questions 6–10, noting which field found each answer.

Homework: for one passage, list every proper noun and every date. Five minutes.
SESSION 3

Add Contrasts, Causes & effects

Warm-up: the five fields so far, from memory. Then the two inference fields, the full timed map (Part 5), and Questions 11–12 plus the reflection questions as the closing task.

Pair with any Cambridge IELTS 15–20 Academic passage that carries a headings block.
TURKISH L1 SPOTLIGHT

“Function” vs “main idea”

Turkish reading instruction leans hard on ana fikir (main idea) and rarely asks what a paragraph's job in the argument is. Students hand you a content summary when a heading needs a functional label — example, counter-argument, consequence.

Drill: before any content notes, force a label from a fixed list — introduces / defines / exemplifies / contrasts / explains cause / evaluates / concludes.

Anticipated problems & solutions

  1. Students copy whole sentences into the cells. This defeats the purpose — the value is in compressing. Cap each cell at eight words; ban any Main idea entry that could be pasted verbatim from the passage.
  2. Every field gets filled, even when the paragraph has nothing for it. Padding hides the signal. Praise a well-judged blank: paragraph A has no Key names, and noticing that is correct reading.
  3. Mapping takes fifteen minutes and there is no time for questions. Expected at first. Speed comes from the staged fields, not from rushing. By session 3 the map is a margin scribble, not a grid.
  4. Students map after reading the questions. Then they only record what they were already looking for. Insist the map is done first, questions covered.

Coaching script

CONFIDENCE ANCHOR — SAY THIS FIRST

“You lose time on Reading because you read the passage again for every single question. Good candidates read it once and take notes. We are going to build the notes. Your English is fine — this is about a system.”

THE MAIN EXPLANATION

“After each paragraph, stop for fifteen seconds. Ask two things: what is this paragraph doing in the text, and what one point is it making? Write both in three or four words. Then, if they are there, grab the names, the dates, the example. That note is what you answer from — not your memory, and not a fourth re-read.”

IF THE STUDENT IS STUCK ON “FUNCTION”

“Do not tell me what it says. Tell me why the writer put it here. Is it an example of the paragraph before? A problem? The writer's opinion? Pick one word.”

ONE NEXT STEP · QUICK CHECK

“This week, every Reading passage you do, write a function label beside each paragraph before you look at the questions. Just the label.” — then check: “Give me the seven fields in order, no notes.”

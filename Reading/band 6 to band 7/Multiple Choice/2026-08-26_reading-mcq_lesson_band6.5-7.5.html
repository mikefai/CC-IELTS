The Elimination Method

Most candidates who lose marks on multiple choice do not misread the passage. They misread the question — and then pick the option that shares the most words with it. This lesson replaces that habit with a procedure.

The habit to build

Before looking at a single option, say out loud what kind of answer the stem demands — "a reason", "a number", "a purpose". Candidates who read the options first are led by them; candidates who predict first use the options as confirmation.

Part 2

The five distractor types

IELTS wrong options are not random. They fail in five recognisable ways. Learn the five and you stop analysing 36 separate options and start recognising five patterns.

TYPE 1

True but not the answer

The statement is accurate according to the passage — it simply does not answer the question that was asked.

Tell: you can find it in the text, but not near the thing the stem asked about.
TYPE 2

Right words, wrong relationship

Vocabulary lifted straight from the passage, but the cause, the date, the agent or the direction has been swapped.

Tell: it feels familiar. Familiarity is the trap, not the evidence.
TYPE 3

Overstatement

The passage hedges — may, some, suggests, probably. The option hardens it into proves, all, always, the first.

Tell: absolute words. Check the passage for a hedge you skipped.
TYPE 4

Outside knowledge

Entirely plausible in the real world, and often actually true — but never stated in this passage.

Tell: you believe it, but you cannot point to a line.
TYPE 5

Half-right

The first half matches the passage perfectly. The second half does not. The deadliest type, because candidates commit after the first clause.

Tell: read every option to its final word before deciding.
THE RULE

An option is wrong until you can point at the line that kills it

"It feels wrong" is not elimination. Name the type. If you cannot name it, you have not eliminated it — you have only disliked it.

Part 3

Elimination strategy

Four options, one answer. That means three of the four contain a specific, findable fault. Hunting the fault is faster and more reliable than hunting the truth, because a fault is usually a single word.

  1. Predict first. From the stem alone, say what the answer must be. Do not look down yet.
  2. Locate the evidence. Find the sentence in the passage. Underline it. You are now answering from the text, not from memory.
  3. Kill on a word. Take each option and find the single word that contradicts, overstates, or misattributes. Cross it out physically.
  4. Compare the survivors. If two remain, do not re-read the passage from the top. Put the two side by side and find the one word where they differ — the answer always turns on that word.
  5. Never leave a blank. No penalty for a wrong answer. A guess between two survivors is a coin flip; a blank is a guaranteed zero.

Part 4 · Option comparison

When two options survive, stop reading the passage and start reading the options. Lay them side by side and mark every word that differs. In a well-made question there is exactly one such word, and the passage decides it. Candidates who instead re-read the whole paragraph lose ninety seconds and usually change a right answer to a wrong one.

Practice · Reading passage

An object out of time

Read the passage, then answer Questions 1–12. Paragraphs are labelled A–G.

An object out of time
A

In the autumn of 1900, a party of Greek sponge divers returning from North Africa was driven by a storm towards the small island of Antikythera, between Crete and the Peloponnese. While waiting for the weather to clear, their captain, Dimitrios Kontos, sent a diver down to survey the seabed. He surfaced with an account of bronze and marble limbs scattered across the slope — the cargo of a ship that had gone down in the first century BCE. Over the following months the divers recovered statues, glassware and coins, and among them an unremarkable corroded lump of bronze roughly the size of a shoebox. It was catalogued, shelved in Athens, and largely ignored.

B

That lump has since become the most discussed object in the history of ancient technology. It survives as 82 fragments containing at least thirty interlocking bronze gears, some with teeth barely more than a millimetre across, originally housed in a wooden case with dials on the front and back and a crank on the side. Turning the crank moved the dials forward or backward in time. What makes the device so difficult to place is not its ingenuity alone but its isolation: no comparable geared mechanism survives from anywhere in the world for more than a thousand years afterwards, until the astronomical clocks of medieval Europe. Nothing in the surviving Greek and Roman literature quite prepares us for it.

C

Dating the device has proved almost as difficult as reading it. The wreck itself is placed in the decades around 70 to 60 BCE on the evidence of the coins and pottery it carried. The mechanism must therefore be at least that old, but how much older is disputed. Estimates based on the style of the lettering have ranged across more than a century, and an argument drawn from the eclipse records inscribed on the back dial points to a starting date of 205 BCE, which would place its design closer to the lifetime of Archimedes than to the wreck itself. No consensus has emerged.

D

Serious investigation began only in 1951, when the historian of science Derek de Solla Price started work on the fragments. Price could not open the device, so he turned to radiography, and in 1971 gamma rays finally revealed the arrangement of gears beneath the corrosion. His monograph Gears from the Greeks, published in 1974, established that the mechanism was a calendrical computer rather than, as some had suggested, a navigational instrument. Price counted the teeth on the surviving gears and worked out several of the ratios. Not all of his conclusions have survived — his reconstruction of the gear train has since been substantially revised — but he was the first to demonstrate what the object fundamentally was.

E

The decisive advance came in 2005, when a team led by the mathematician and film-maker Tony Freeth brought microfocus X-ray computed tomography to Athens, together with a surface-imaging technique that exaggerates the play of light across a corroded face. The scans resolved individual gear teeth in three dimensions. More unexpectedly, they exposed some 3,400 characters of Greek text inscribed on the plates — effectively an instruction manual, most of it never read before. The inscriptions confirmed that the back dials tracked the nineteen-year Metonic cycle, which reconciles lunar months with solar years, and the Saros cycle, by which eclipses repeat approximately every eighteen years.

F

The most striking finding concerns the Moon. Its apparent speed across the sky is not constant, a variation Greek astronomers had described but could not easily reproduce mechanically. The engineer Michael Wright, who has built several working reconstructions, identified the solution: one gear carries a pin that rides in a slot cut into a second gear mounted slightly off-centre. As the pair rotates, the pin alternately speeds and slows the second gear. It is an elegant piece of engineering, and it means the maker was not merely counting days but modelling the irregular behaviour of a body in the sky. Wright has argued that the front of the device probably carried pointers for the five planets known in antiquity as well, though the relevant fragments do not survive.

G

Further scans in 2008 identified a small subsidiary dial listing the four-year cycle of athletic games, Olympia among them — a domestic touch on an otherwise astronomical instrument. The deeper question remains unanswered. A device of this refinement is unlikely to have been a first attempt, and Cicero, writing within a century of the wreck, twice describes bronze spheres that reproduced the motions of the Sun, Moon and planets. The probability is that the Antikythera mechanism belonged to a tradition of instrument-making that was substantial in its own time and has left almost nothing else behind. What survives is not a marvel without context, but the single surviving page of a lost literature.

15:00

On the 15:00 timer. That is lesson pace — about 75 seconds a question on a passage you have already read. It is not exam pace. In the real test you get roughly 20 minutes for a passage and its whole question block, and the working rule is: if one question passes 90 seconds, mark it and move on. Never lose three easy questions chasing one hard one.

Answer key

Every answer, and why every wrong option fails

Each wrong option is tagged with its distractor type from Part 2. The point is not to learn twelve answers — it is to start seeing the same five faults everywhere.

Marking note

Twelve questions is not a band score. Use the count diagnostically: if the misses cluster in one distractor type, that type is the lesson for next week. A candidate who repeatedly falls for Type 5 · Half-right needs a reading-to-the-final-word drill, not more passages.

For the teacher

How to run this

This page contains four strategies. That is more than one lesson can carry — working memory holds about four new chunks at once, and a session that introduces four strategies consolidates none of them. Split it.

Three-session split

SESSION 1

Stem analysis

Part 1 only. Students dissect ten stems from any Cambridge passage, naming the four parts. Then Questions 1–4 here, untimed, with the key open.

Homework: label the parts of five stems. Five minutes, not fifty.
SESSION 2

The five distractor types

Retrieval warm-up first: name the four stem parts from memory, no notes. Then Part 2, then Questions 5–8 — tagging every rejected option with a type number.

Homework: find one real example of Type 5 in any practice book.
SESSION 3

Elimination and comparison

Warm-up: the five types from memory. Then Parts 3–4, Questions 9–12, then the timed retry across all twelve as the closing task.

Pair with any Cambridge IELTS 15–20 Academic passage carrying an MCQ block.
TURKISH L1 SPOTLIGHT

Under-reading the hedge

English carries hedging lexically — may, suggests, probably, is unlikely to. Turkish carries it grammatically, through -DIr and evidential -mIş. Students therefore read straight past English hedges, which is precisely what Type 3 · Overstatement exploits.

Drill separately: highlight every hedge in a paragraph before answering anything.

Coaching script

CONFIDENCE ANCHOR — SAY THIS FIRST

“You already do the hard part — you understand the passage. What is costing you marks is not your English. It is that you pick the option that looks most like the text. That is a habit, and habits we can change in a week.”

THE MAIN EXPLANATION

“Four options, one is right. So three of them have a fault you can find. Do not hunt for the right answer — hunt for the fault. Cover the options with your hand. Read the question. Say out loud what kind of answer you need: a reason? a number? a purpose? Now uncover them, and for each wrong one put your finger on the word that kills it. If you cannot find the word, you have not eliminated it. You have just decided you do not like it.”

IF THE STUDENT IS STUCK

“Forget four. Take just these two. Which one is wrong — and which single word tells you?”

IF THE STUDENT IS DISCOURAGED

“You got four wrong. Now look at which type they were. All Type 5? Then you do not have four problems, you have one — you are deciding before the end of the sentence. That is a two-minute fix, not a two-month one.”

ONE NEXT STEP · QUICK CHECK

“This week, every multiple-choice question you do, write the type number beside each option you cross out. Just the number. Nothing else.” — then check: “Give me the five types without looking.”

Scope note

Twelve questions on one passage is a technique drill, not a test. A full IELTS Academic Reading paper is three passages and forty questions in sixty minutes, and no band should be reported from this alone. Use the count diagnostically: if the misses cluster in one distractor type, that type is next week's lesson.

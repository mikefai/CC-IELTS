The rise of urban beekeeping 1 Twenty years ago, beehives in cities were a rarity, kept only by a handful of enthusiasts willing to argue with neighbours and landlords. Today, municipal rooftops from London to Toronto host thousands of colonies, and waiting lists for urban beekeeping courses routinely exceed capacity. The revival has been driven partly by concern for pollinators, whose numbers have fallen sharply in agricultural regions, and partly by a desire among city residents to produce some of their own food. 2 Cities, it turns out, suit bees remarkably well. Urban parks, balconies and roadside planters provide a wider variety of flowers across a longer season than the monoculture fields of modern farming, where a single crop flowers for a few weeks and is then treated with pesticide. Studies in Paris found city bees produced more honey per hive than their rural counterparts, largely because ornamental plants bloom from early spring to late autumn. 3 The trend is not without critics. Some ecologists warn that honeybees — essentially farmed animals — may out-compete wild bees and other pollinators, which are the species genuinely at risk. A hive of fifty thousand honeybees can consume the nectar that would otherwise support hundreds of wild pollinators in the same area. Several European cities have therefore begun limiting the number of hives per rooftop rather than encouraging more. 4 For urban beekeepers themselves, the greatest challenge is not the bees but the bureaucracy. Insurance requirements, neighbour consultation rules and rooftop access restrictions vary from district to district, and a hive that is perfectly legal on one street may require permits on the next. Associations of beekeepers have responded by creating shared guidelines, and some city councils now offer free registration and training to bring hives into the open.
